## 3 · Finding the Raven

They take the king away to be washed.

That is what happens here after a man dies in your room, I learn: two priests with shaved heads and a bowl of natron come in, and a physician, and the king is lifted up off the floor by people who do not ask him, and carried out through a door I hadn't noticed to be purified of something. A serving girl is already on her knees with a cloth and a bucket. The water in the bucket goes pink and then brown. The red bird on the wall stays red.

"Go," Tut says to me over the physician's shoulder, in his flat voice. "Tomorrow."

Aneb walks me out.

He doesn't walk too fast this time. He walks like a man who has just run at a knife and is still finding out whether his legs are his. In the corridor he stops, and I stop, and the bar of light from a window slit lies between us on the floor.

"I need to ask you something," I say.

"Ask the king. You're his."

"The king is nine."

That gets me a look. The scar pulls his face slightly to one side when he frowns, so that half of him is angry and the other half is only tired.

"Who in this palace knows how it works," I say, "and has no reason to lie to a toy?"

He doesn't answer right away. I watch him decide whether I am worth the breath.

"The Raven," he says finally. "Go and ask the Raven. He likes strays." He jerks his chin down the corridor. "Second court, past the kitchens. The House of Life. Follow the ink. If the ink runs out, follow the wine."

"Who is the Raven?"

"The old king's pet." He's already walking away, back toward the room with the red bird in it. "You two should get along."

*He wants me away from the king,* I think, watching his back. *And he's just told me something true to do it. Both of those are useful.*

I go to find the Raven.

***

Two hundred and forty steps. Seven turns. The kitchens announce themselves before I get to them, smoke and onions and a woman's voice telling someone he is the son of a donkey, and after the kitchens the corridor narrows and the walls stop being painted and start being just walls.

The House of Life is a long low room with a garden on the other side of its windows. Shelves run from floor to ceiling, and on the shelves are jars, and in the jars are rolls of papyrus, hundreds of them, each with a little tag of leather hanging out. The dust is the dust of things that have been written down and then left alone. It smells of ink and reed and, under that, sour wine.

One man is in it.

He sits cross-legged at a low table with a roll open in front of him and a reed pen in his hand, and he is copying something, line by line, from an older roll onto a newer one. He is a big man, broad in the shoulders in a way that doesn't go with the pen, and he stoops over the table as if whatever he is carrying on his back has been there a long time. His hair is gray, and he isn't old enough for it to be gray, and it's tied back in a soldier's tail. His feet are bare. His fingers are black with ink to the second knuckle.

His left eye is a seam of scar tissue, closed for good. The right one is green.

There is a cup of wine by his elbow. It's the middle of the afternoon.

"The king's toy," he says, without lifting his head. His voice is smooth and low and formal, the voice of a man reading aloud from something written a long time ago. "Did he lose you? Or did you lose him?"

"Neither, my lord. I was told you know this palace."

"Who told you?"

"A slave."

"Then you've learned one thing already, and I didn't teach it to you." The pen moves. "The slaves know everything, and nobody asks them."

I wait. He lets me wait. He finishes a line, dips the pen, starts another.

"The pharaoh's court devours the naive," he says to the papyrus. "If you wish to outlast the boy who had your room before you, you will have to learn to smile while the knife finds another man's back. It is a thing that can be learned. I don't know that it can be taught." Now the green eye comes up and finds me, and it does not go up and down me the way everyone else's has. It goes straight to my face and stays. "And why would I teach a foreign child? What is in it for me? A child forgets by supper. A foreign child is dead by the flood."

"I don't want to learn to smile."

"No?"

"I want to know why a man with a knife stops."

The pen stops.

He sets it down across the roll, carefully, so the ink won't run, and he puts his two black hands flat on the table, and he looks at me with his one eye for the time it takes the dust to turn over once in the window light.

"Tell me what you saw."

So I tell him. The curtain. The man who didn't shout, who held the knife along his forearm like someone who had used one. How he came across the room at a run with no sound but sandals. How the point stopped a hand's width from the gold collar, and the man's eyes went to the door, and then the Commander's blade came, in that order and not the other.

"A man who means it doesn't stop, my lord. He finishes. A man who stops wants to be seen trying."

"Seen by whom?"

"I don't know."

"And who did they blame?"

"Nobody has said."

"Nobody will say it to you. By supper it will be a Hittite. Or a man of the Heretic's household. Or a priest of some god nobody has prayed to for a generation. Whichever name is useful this month." He picks up the cup, looks into it, puts it down without drinking. "So. Which name is useful this month?"

"I don't know that either."

"Good. You've said *I don't know* three times, and you are the first person in this room in a year who has. Everyone else who comes in here knows everything." He leans back. "The man is dead. His blood is real. Did the king's heart beat faster?"

I think of the cone, set down carefully in the blood on the square.

"No."

"No. Because it was the third this season. One in the garden, one in the bath, and now one at the game board, and each one a man nobody knew, cut down before he could say who sent him. Does a play need to be false to work, child? Or does it only need to be repeated?"

"Then what is it for?"

"What is fear for?"

I don't answer that. I've been afraid for two days. I know exactly what it's for.

"Who taught you," he says, "to watch a man's eyes while he dies?"

"No one. I watched."

"Where did you learn to speak like this? Herders don't."

"I listened to old men."

He laughs, once, a short bark that doesn't reach the eye. "That I believe." He turns the copper ring on his finger. "Why do you want to live? Everyone who comes through that hall wants not to die. It isn't the same thing. Why do *you* want to live?"

*Because the god gave me two years and I'm not spending them on a mat behind a bar. Because there's a boy on a throne who checks the door before he laughs and I know how old he'll be when they put him in the ground. Because my mother said a thing to me with her last breath and I still don't know what it meant.*

"Because I owe a debt," I say.

He looks at me a long time.

"Everyone in Kemet owes a debt," he says. "The river collects. The Nile remembers what men forget." He picks up the pen again. "Come to the hall tonight. The court dines when the lamps are lit. Sit where the king puts you. Watch where I look. If you can tell me afterward what you saw, I will teach you. If you can't, then you'll have learned something anyway, and it won't matter, because you'll be dead by the flood."

"And the price, my lord?"

"Ah. He asks the price." The pen goes back to the line it left. "I don't take students for nothing. The price is this. When I ask you a question, you answer it true. Lie to the king. Lie to the Vizier. Lie to the gods, if you can find one that's listening. Not to me."

"Yes, my lord."

I am going to break that before the lamps are lit. I know it as I say it. I've been lying since I woke up in the alley, and the lie is the only thing keeping the Weighing of the Heart on the other side of the door. I'll lie to this man too. I'll just have to be better at it than I've been at losing.

"They call you the Raven," I say at the door.

"They call me many things. That is the one they say to my face." He doesn't look up. "Two hundred and forty steps back, child. Seven turns. Try not to be killed on any of them."

I stop with my hand on the frame.

I hadn't said the number out loud. I'm certain I hadn't.

"Go," says the Raven of Kemet, and dips his pen.
