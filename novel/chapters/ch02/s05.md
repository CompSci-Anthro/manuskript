## 5 · The First Lesson

Nakhti's room is three turns past the House of Life and no bigger than mine. A mat, a lamp, a shelf of rolls, a jar of wine on the floor with a cup beside it. On the wall above the mat, hung on two pegs where another man would hang a god, is a bronze dagger with a crack running through the blade from the point to the hilt.

He doesn't explain it. I don't ask.

He pours wine and drinks half of it standing and sits down on the floor with the cup, and I see the thing in him that the wine is for, and I put that in the ledger too, on his page, which I have just started.

"Sit," he says. "Not there. Where you can see the door."

I move.

He takes a handful of date stones out of a dish and sets one on the floor between us, alone, in the circle of the lamp.

"The king."

Another, long and thin, close beside it. Closer than any of the others will be.

"The Vizier. He holds the king's seal in everything but his hand. Every order in Kemet goes through him. Grain, soldiers, judges, the dead. He was a scribe once. Men who were scribes know that whoever holds the pen holds what is true."

A third stone, on the other side of the king.

"The High Priest of Amun."

"What is his name?"

"Names are paid for in this palace, child. You will have his when you can afford it." He sets a fourth stone, fat, a little apart. "Userhat. The treasury. He wanted your head last night because the Vizier wanted to see whether the king would give it. The king didn't. Userhat will remember that you cost him something. Fat men remember."

A fifth, by the door of the circle, standing up on its end.

"Nakhtmin. The Shield of the Living Pharaoh. Three hundred men in the palace and the only blades inside these walls that aren't the Vizier's. He takes his orders from the Vizier, as the Vizier takes his from the king, as the king takes his from the gods. Ask yourself which of those is true."

"Does he hate it?"

"Ask me again in a year."

Two more stones, far outside the lamplight, almost at the wall.

"Horemheb. Great Commander of the Army, in the north, with every soldier in Kemet who has ever seen a Hittite. He has not come south in two years. And Ay. God's Father. He taught the old king's children their letters and he keeps to his estates at Akhmim and he says nothing. Silence is also a side. Remember that one."

*Ay.* On the chart over my bed, in my mother's handwriting, the name after Tut's. The man who gets the throne when the boy is nineteen. I look at the little stone by the wall and I feel the cold go through me that I felt in the hall when the herald read out the cartouche.

*I know how this ends. I'm the only one in the room who does, and it's the one thing I can never say.*

"Land," Nakhti says, and draws a line through the dust with his finger around the priest's stone, a big circle. "The temple of Amun holds a third of the fields in the Black Land since the restoration, and the temple's grain goes where the High Priest says." A smaller circle around the king and the Vizier together. "The king's fields. The Vizier administers them. The nomarchs in the provinces owe both and love neither." He taps the standing stone. "Soldiers, you've seen. Temples, you've seen. That is the whole of the palace. Three things, and who holds them."

"And you, my lord?"

"Me." He picks up a stone and holds it, and then puts it back in the dish instead of on the floor. "The old king found me in a slave gang on a canal and decided I could count. He made me a scribe, and then a steward, and then an advisor, and the whole court has spent ten years waiting for him to be proved wrong. He's dead. They're still waiting." He drinks. "Now listen, because I'll say this once and you'll hear it every day of your life after."

He holds up one black finger.

"Never show your real thoughts. You failed that tonight, before the whole court, and a widow was still saying thank you when you did it."

A second finger.

"Never trust beauty or kindness. The Vizier was kind to a widow tonight. You saw what the kindness cost. Everything beautiful in this palace was put there to be looked at while something else happens."

A third.

"Never forget the cost of each lie you tell. You're going to tell a great many, child. I can see it on you. Every one of them is a stone you carry, and one day you'll be thrown in the river, and you'll find out what you weigh."

*Three,* I think. *I've already broken the third one with you, and we're not an hour in.*

"Now. Two Egypts live in this palace." He sweeps his hand once over the stones, dividing them. "The Vizier's. The seal, the treasury, the scribes, the Medjay in the city, and the priesthood of Amun leaning his way like a wall leans before it goes over. That Egypt is the one with the grain in it." The hand comes back. "The king's. The king. A slave with a burned face. Three hundred guards under a commander whose orders come from the other side. A few old servants who remember his father. Me." The green eye lifts. "And you. Whether you want it or not. Because the king put his hand on your head tonight in front of everyone, and the Vizier saw him do it, and in this palace a thing that is seen has happened."

"Who do I beware of?"

"Everyone. But if you want a list: Userhat, who is stupid and owed. The Vizier's steward, who has soft hands and does hard things with them. The servant with the split lip, whom you've met. The priests who come to purify a room after a death, because they also come before." He pauses, and turns the copper ring. "And a man who comes and goes from the Vizier's house at night with his face covered, who smells of myrrh and something burnt. I don't know his name. No one does. If you ever smell him, child, walk the other way."

"And who do I trust?"

"No one."

"You?"

"I'm the one teaching you not to." He almost smiles. "Hori, then. The soldier who pulled you out of the river and fed you. A man who feeds a prisoner is either kind or ordered. Find out which before you eat again."

I look at the stones for a while. The lamp hisses. Somewhere in the palace a door closes, and I count the echo, four, which means a courtyard, which means the second court, which means the way I came.

"Why does the Vizier need the attempts to be seen?"

"Does he?"

"The man stopped and looked at the door. He wanted an audience. Three this season, and all three cut down by the Commander before they could speak, and the Commander answers to the Vizier." I'm talking faster than I should. I can hear it. "A king who is afraid signs what he's handed. Every attempt needs a name, and every name the Vizier gives it is a man he's removed. And every time, it's the Vizier's commander who saved the king, so every time, it was the Vizier who saved the king. He doesn't need the king dead. He needs the king to need him."

Nakhti doesn't say anything.

"Who told you that?" he says, finally.

"You did. You didn't say it."

"The gods whisper to those who listen," he says, slowly. "Wisdom hides in silence, not in shouting. You listen well, child. You shout a little. We'll work on it." He drinks. "And the councilor? Since you've been so busy."

"Kanefer." I'd heard the wife say it, over and over, before she stopped. "He asked a question in council this morning."

"Did he."

"You said a man who asks a question dies. The fan-bearer asked one. I'd guess his was about grain, since the Vizier's Egypt is the one with the grain in it, and the temple's circle on your floor is bigger than the king's."

The green eye doesn't blink.

"He asked why the temple of Amun's tallies have grown for three floods," Nakhti says, "while the army's have shrunk, with Hatti at the door of Djahy."

"Three floods."

"Three."

"Since the old king died."

He goes very still. The lamp hisses. The cracked dagger on the wall throws a shadow with a crack in it.

"Since the old king died," he says, and it isn't a question, and I understand that I've just stepped on something I can't see the shape of yet, a thing under the sand that this man has been walking around for three years with his eye on the ground.

He sets down the cup. He looks at me the way he looked at me in the House of Life when the pen stopped, straight at the face, not up and down, and I let him, and I think of the three things he just told me never to do, and I keep my real thoughts behind my eyes, where they belong, and I say nothing.

It is the first thing I've done right all day.

"You have the body of a child," says the Raven of Kemet, "but you listen like a man who has already lost something. Wisdom like that is rare. And in this palace, it's dangerous."
