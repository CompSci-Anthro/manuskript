# Chapter 2 — The Shield of the Pharaoh

## 1 · Games in the Royal Chambers

"Sit with me. Sit with me."

The Pharaoh of the Two Lands, Lord of the Thrones, Nebkheperure Tutankhamun, is patting the floor beside him with one small hand the way you'd call a cat.

I sit.

It is two hundred and twelve steps from my room to this one, with four turns, two of them left. I counted them behind Aneb's back while he walked too fast on purpose. A slave who is a head and a half taller than the king's new pet, and who wanted the pet to trot. I didn't trot. I counted.

*They see a child. Let them see a slow one.*

The bedchamber is the first soft place I have found in this palace. The walls are painted with reeds and birds, a marsh that goes all the way around the room, so the bed sits in the middle of it like a boat. A real bed, on carved lion's feet, with linen heaped on it. Heavy curtains hang over the far wall from ceiling to floor. The afternoon comes in through the high windows in long bars, and dust turns inside them, slow and gold.

Two guards stand on either side of the door. They haven't moved since I came in. I've been timing their breathing.

"You know senet," Tut says.

"No, Majesty."

"Good." He pushes the board between us. "Learn slowly."

He said it to me last night with a rope on my wrists and a dead man's blood still drying on the floor between us. He says it now the same way, flat, like a thing that has been true for a long time.

The board is a box of dark wood with thirty squares cut into the lid, three rows of ten. Five pieces each, his cones and my spools. He throws the sticks to show me, four flat sticks that clatter and come up two dark, two pale.

"Two," he says, and moves a cone.

I throw. Three. I move the wrong piece, and he reaches over and moves the right one for me without a word.

Aneb has not sat down. He stands at the head of the bed with his arms folded, and he watches me the way you'd watch a strange dog that has gotten near a baby.

He is the one who came for me at noon. He is tall and lean and dark, with black hair parted to one side, and a burn scar that runs across his face from his left temple to the right corner of his jaw, pale as a cut in bark. His linen is undyed and plain. No collar, no ring. Nothing on him that anyone would bother to steal. When he opened my door he said one word, "Boy," and then turned and walked, and that was the whole of our conversation.

He's eighteen, I'd guess. My age.

I lose the first game in a very long time, because it takes me most of it to understand the rules.

I lose the second game in exactly the right amount of time, and that is harder.

There are only three things you need to know about senet. The sticks decide how far you go; the board decides where it hurts; the rest is counting, and counting is the one thing this body didn't take from me. By the middle of the second game I can see four throws ahead, and the whole problem becomes losing in a way that looks like a boy who is trying.

I let him send my spool back to the House of Water. He laughs.

It comes out of him all at once, a high crack of a laugh, and he claps his hand over his mouth as if someone might hear it from the hall and come to take it away. His eyes go to the door. Then to Aneb. Then, slowly, back to the board.

"Again," he says.

*A nine-year-old,* I think, *who checks the door before he laughs.*

That is the thing I keep forgetting, and that I can't afford to forget: nobody in this room is what they look like. He looks like a little boy in a gold collar with his thumbnail bitten to the quick. He is a god, or the Two Lands say he is, and a god with a seal. Last night that seal was the only thing between me and the scale.

He throws. He moves. He bends over the board with the whole of his small body, and under the gold collar he is thin, narrow across the shoulders, built out of wire and stubbornness. There are shadows under his eyes that don't belong on a face that age.

I know those shadows. I had them at ten too. Mine came from reading under the blanket with a flashlight. I don't think his did.

A dish comes in on the hands of a serving girl who keeps her eyes on the floor: a bowl of roasted quail eggs, small and speckled, with a saucer of salt and crushed cumin beside them. She sets it down between us and goes out backward.

Tut takes one, rolls it in the salt, and eats it in two bites.

I don't touch them.

He notices on the third egg. He notices most things, I'm learning. He just doesn't say.

"You don't eat."

"I am waiting for Your Majesty to finish."

"Why?"

*Because the last boy who sat here lasted eleven days, and nobody has told me how.*

"Where I come from, Majesty, a guest waits."

"Where you come from," Aneb says from the bed, "they have hair like wheat and the manners of a scribe's grandfather, and nobody has ever heard of it."

"East," I say, without turning around. "Herders. A long way."

"Herders." He makes the word sound like something stuck to a sandal.

"I was robbed on the road to the city. I came here with nothing."

"You came here with a piece of foreign paper and a mouth."

"Aneb." Tut doesn't look up from the board. It isn't loud. It's barely a word at all.

Aneb shuts his mouth. His jaw works once, and he looks away at the painted marsh on the wall as if he's counting the birds.

"Aneb tastes everything," Tut tells me. "Before it comes to me. Since I was four."

I look at the tall slave with the ruined face, who has just been told to be quiet by a child, and who did it. He has been putting strange food in his mouth for this boy for five years, every dish, every day, waiting to find out whether he dies. There is a kind of loyalty that is only a different word for being owned. I don't know yet which kind his is.

"Then he has tasted," I say. "And Your Majesty has eaten. And now I will."

I take an egg. It's good. It's the first thing I've eaten since the cherry road.

I didn't eat Hori's bread. I sat up all night with it on the mat beside me, listening to the bar on the door and the grain falling in the glass against my hip, and when the window slit went gray I scratched a line on the wall beside the last boy's ten and a half, and I rubbed out the map I'd drawn in the sand under the mat, and I was still hungry. Hungry is better than eleven days.

*One. Seven hundred and twenty-nine to go.*

I eat a second egg because the first one was fine and because Tut is watching me do it, and something in his face eases, as if I'd passed a test he didn't know he was giving.

We play again. I throw a four and move a spool onto a square that lets him trap it, and he sees it, and his mouth twitches. I'm getting better at losing. It's a skill. I had a fencing coach who said the hardest thing to teach was a boy who could already hit, how to miss on purpose so the other boy would come in. I was that boy. I'm that boy again, except now the other boy is a king and the point of the game is my throat.

While he thinks, I look at the room.

Under the heaped linen at the foot of the bed, half hidden, is a small carved boat. Wood, old, the paint worn off where a hand has held it. The only toy in a room built for a king.

High in the ceiling, in the corner over the curtains, a bronze grate covers a vent. A square of darker dark. The lamps aren't lit yet, and the daylight doesn't reach it.

The guards at the door breathe nine times a minute. I've been through it twice.

Tut moves his last cone off the board and sits back on his heels.

"I win."

"Your Majesty wins."

"You lost slower that time."

"I am learning slowly, Majesty. As I was told."

He looks at me for a while with his dark eyes, and I let him look. Then he takes another egg and rolls it in the salt, and the corner of his mouth goes up, and he doesn't check the door first.

Something in my chest I haven't felt since the fire lets go a little.

*Two years,* I think. *Seven hundred and twenty-nine days of this. A floor to sit on. A boy who wants to play. A game I can count.* I have survived worse rooms. I survived a six-mat room over a dry cleaner's with the rent paid by the ward office. Maybe this is what the god meant. Maybe a second childhood doesn't have to be the first one over again.

The afternoon light has climbed the far wall and gone orange on the curtains.

Behind the curtains, where there is no door, a shadow moves.
