## 4 · A Common Occurrence

The soldier who comes for me at lamplight is Hori.

I know him by the way he fills the door: the one who pulled me out of the river mud with one hand, who whispered the court's names at my shoulder with a rope in his fist, who dropped a loaf on my mat and told me how long the last boy lasted. He looks at me as if I'm a parcel he's been told to carry twice.

"The king sends for his pet."

"You brought the bread," I say.

"I was told to."

That's all. He walks and I follow and I count, and I think about the two ways a man can be told to feed a prisoner.

***

The court dines in a long hall of columns with the lamps already burning, hundreds of them, in bronze dishes along the walls and on stands between the low tables, so the whole room is the color of the inside of a honey jar. Lotus flowers float in bowls of water on every table. Three harpists sit in a ring of light at the far end and play something slow that goes around and around. The air is roast goose and figs and perfume cones melting on the heads of the guests, sweet fat and wax, and under all of it the myrrh, which I love, which I will never again be able to smell without thinking of this night.

The king sits at the head of the hall on a raised seat with a footrest his feet don't reach.

I sit where he puts me, on a cushion on the floor at his feet, like a dog that has been allowed into the house. Aneb stands behind the seat and takes a piece of everything off the king's dish before it is set down, chews it with his eyes on the middle distance, and waits. When nothing happens the king eats.

On the king's right hand, closer to him than any man in the hall, sits the Vizier.

I haven't seen Amenhotep since the hall last night. He is longer, sitting down, than most men standing: a tall thin shape in indigo linen with gold Amun signs along the hem, a collar of lapis and red stone, his shaved head banded once in gold. His hands are wrapped in strips of linen, like a man with burns, and the one thumbnail that shows is black to the root. He talks to the king in a voice too soft for me to hear, with long pauses in it, and the king nods into his plate.

He hasn't looked at me. He hasn't looked at me once, and I can feel the not-looking like a hand near the back of my neck.

Beyond him, the High Priest, lean, shaved, a leopard skin over one shoulder, counting breaths between his words. Beyond him the fat official who demanded my head last night, Userhat, sucking the meat off a bone with his eyes closed. Along the left wall, standing, Nakhtmin and a line of his guards, spears upright, still as the columns.

And by the door, at the lowest table in the hall, with the servants coming and going past his back, the Raven of Kemet, alone, with a cup.

He hadn't turned around when Hori walked me past him. He had been looking at his wine. But he'd said it, low, in the voice of a man reading from an old roll, as I passed within a foot of his shoulder:

"Watch this common occurrence."

So I watch.

I watch where he looks, which is the first thing. He is not looking at the king, or the Vizier, or the priest. He is looking two tables down the right-hand side, where a man of about forty with a green-striped collar is talking with both his hands. A broad face, a loud laugh, a wife beside him half his age with a lotus in her hair, who keeps touching his arm as if to slow him down.

Cup-bearers move along the tables with tall jars on their hips and fill cups as they go. One of them fills the Vizier's cup. One fills Userhat's. The High Priest covers his with his palm, and the bearer passes on.

The man with the green-striped collar isn't filled from a jar.

His cup comes to him on a small tray from the side of the hall, carried by a servant with a split lip badly healed, and is set down at his hand already full. He doesn't look at it. He's talking. His wife looks at it, and then at the servant, who is already gone back behind a column, and then at nothing.

The Vizier looks at him. Once, mildly, the way you'd check on bread in an oven.

The High Priest looks at the Vizier.

*The same look,* I think. *Before he read the scale.*

The man with the green collar finishes his sentence and laughs at it and picks up his cup and drinks half of it down.

Nothing happens for a while. The harps go around. Aneb tastes a fig. The Vizier says something to the king with a long pause in the middle of it, and the king nods.

Then the man puts his hand on the table.

Just that. Flat on the table, fingers spread, as if the room had moved. His wife says something to him. He doesn't answer. He tries to stand, and gets halfway up, and his legs go out from under him as if the strings had been cut, and he sits down hard and the table jumps and the bowl of lotus flowers slops water over the edge.

His hand goes to his throat.

He makes a sound. It's not a word. It's a sound a man makes when he has forgotten how his breathing works and is trying to remember, a long dragging pull that comes up short and tries again. His face is going dark, dark red going toward purple, and his eyes are very wide and very white in it. There is foam at the corner of his mouth. His wife has both hands on his arm now and is saying his name, and then she is saying it louder, and then she looks around the hall for someone to help her.

Nobody is looking.

I mean that exactly. Userhat is eating. The man across from him is eating. A woman three tables up laughs at something, a real laugh, with her head back. The harpists play. The cup-bearers walk their rounds. Nakhtmin stands at the wall with his face like a shut door and does not move his eyes from the far end of the room.

The man's back arches. Both his heels come off the floor and drum on it, once, twice, and his arm sweeps the table and the bronze bowl goes over and the water runs out across the wood and the lotus flowers go with it, white and pink, turning slowly in the spill, and he goes down face first into the middle of them.

He shakes for a while. Then he doesn't.

The flowers go on turning.

Two servants come from behind the columns without hurrying, and lift him under the arms, and carry him out the side door of the hall with his heels dragging, the way the assassin's arms dragged this afternoon. His wife stands where she is with the water running off the table onto her feet.

Then the Vizier rises.

He is the tallest man in the room and the thinnest and he rises like something uncoiling, and his shadow goes up the column behind him into the dark. He walks the length of the right-hand side of the hall, unhurried, with the harps playing him down it, and he stops in front of the widow and takes both her hands in his wrapped ones and bends his long body down to her.

He smiles at her. It is a warm smile. I'm thirty feet away and I can feel the warmth of it.

He says something I can't hear. It has pauses in it. She stares at him, and then her face crumples, and she nods and nods, and she says, "Thank you, my lord." She thanks him. She thanks him twice.

*Write it down,* I tell myself. *All of it. The cup. The split lip. The look. The smile. The thank you.* I keep a ledger. I have kept it since the fire. It has a page for everyone who has ever decided something about my life without asking me, and the Vizier of the Two Lands has just gone onto it in ink that doesn't wash.

I'm watching the side door, where the servant with the split lip went, when I understand what my face has been doing.

Nothing.

A child would have cried out. A child would have hidden his face in the king's knee, or been sick on the floor, or screamed when the heels drummed. Every child in this hall, and there are three or four, is being held by a mother or has been hurried out. I have sat on my cushion with my hands in my lap and watched a man die for the third time in two days, and I have watched it like a man reading, and when I look up from the side door the Vizier is looking at me.

Across the whole length of the hall. Still holding the widow's hands.

His eyes are hazel, and in the lamplight they have gone to gold, and they rest on me the way they rested on the man with the green collar, mildly, checking the oven. They don't move on. The harps go around. The widow is still saying thank you.

Then he smiles at me.

It is exactly the same smile.

*He has read past the child,* I think. *All the way to the small print.*

A hand comes down on top of my head.

Small, warm, a little sticky with honey. The king's. He doesn't look at me. He's looking at his dish, and his other hand is on the black stone scarab at his collar, the one he touches when he's afraid, and the hand on my hair moves once, the way you'd pat a dog that has been good, so that everyone in the hall who was watching the Vizier watch me now sees a boy with a king's hand on his head.

A pet.

The gold eyes rest on us both for one more breath. Then the Vizier straightens, and lets go of the widow, and walks back up the hall to his seat, and the harps go around, and I sit under the hand of a nine-year-old who has just, without looking at me, put himself between me and the only man in Egypt who frightens him, and I don't know yet whether to be grateful or afraid.

Both. The answer is both. It's always going to be both.

***

The Raven is waiting in the dark of the corridor outside, where the lamps don't reach.

"Well?"

I tell him. The cup that came full. The split lip. The look from the priest to the Vizier, the same look as at the scale. The way nobody stopped eating. The smile. The two thank yous. The hand on my head.

"Why did the king do that?"

I've been thinking about it the whole way down the hall.

"To make me a pet again," I say. "In front of the Vizier. Because the Vizier had stopped seeing one."

Nakhti is quiet for a long time. Somewhere behind us the harps have started a new song that sounds like the old one.

"This is the truth of the royal palace," he says. "Come. You've seen it. Now I'll tell you what it means."
