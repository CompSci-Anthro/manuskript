## 2 · The Assassin

The curtain opens the way a mouth opens.

A man comes out of it. Bare chest, a servant's kilt, palace sandals, a knife held point down along his forearm the way a man holds a knife when he has used one before. He does not shout. He does not say the name of a god. He comes across the painted marsh at a run, and the only sound is his sandals on the plaster.

I have been in rooms with blades for nine years. Tournaments, salles, a coach who liked to come in low and fast to see if you'd flinch. My body knows what to do when steel comes at it. It knows how to give ground on the back foot, how to take the line, how to make the other man's speed into his mistake.

My body is ten years old.

Nothing happens. I sit on the floor with a wooden spool in my fingers and I watch him come, and my legs are two sticks somebody else owns.

*Move.*

I don't.

The guards at the door turn their heads. Both of them, together, like a thing on a hinge. Aneb is already running, from the bed, with his hands empty and his mouth open, and he is too far, and he knows it.

Tut doesn't look up.

He is holding one cone between two fingers over the square he was going to move to. He looks at the board. The man's shadow goes over him and over the board and over me.

The knife comes down toward the gold collar.

And stops.

A hand's width from the boy's chest, the point stops in the air, and the man's eyes go, not to the king, but past him, to the door.

Then the door is full of Nakhtmin.

I don't see him cross the room. One moment the door is empty and the next the Commander of the Royal Retainers is there with a khopesh in his hand, the curved bronze catching the orange light, and the next moment after that the blade has gone in under the man's jaw and across.

It is not quick. I thought it would be quick.

The man's head goes back. The knife falls out of his fingers and bounces once on the senet board and sends the cones everywhere. He puts both hands to his throat as if he could hold it closed. He can't. It comes through his fingers, dark and thick and more of it than you would think a man had, and it goes down his chest and onto the plaster, and onto the painted reeds, and a bird on the wall behind him is suddenly red.

He goes down on his knees first. Then onto his side, across the board, with his face a foot from mine.

He is trying to breathe. The sound it makes is a sound I will hear for a long time, a wet pulling, in and in and in, and bubbles at the cut. His eyes are open. He's looking at me, or at the place where I am. His sandal heel scrapes the floor, scrapes it, scrapes it, slower.

I watch him the whole way. I watched the old man last night and I watch this one now. It seems like the least I can do.

It takes him about as long as a game of senet.

Then the heel stops.

Nakhtmin wipes the khopesh on the dead man's kilt, twice, one side and then the other. He is broad through the shoulders and young, younger than the Vizier's officers, with a scar along his jaw and black hair pulled back so hard it looks painful. His eyes are older than the rest of him. They go once around the room, the curtain, the window, the grate, the guards, me, and come to rest on the king.

"Majesty."

"I'm not hurt."

"Majesty." A breath. "The Vizier will want to see him."

"Take him, then."

"It will be done."

He says it like a door closing. The two guards come off the wall at last and take the dead man by the ankles. They drag him out through the door and his arms trail behind him over his head, and his open eyes go over the threshold last, and the blood makes a wide smooth road behind him across the floor.

Nakhtmin goes to the curtain and pulls it back. Behind it there is a narrow door in the plaster, plain, unpainted, servants' height. He looks at it for a while. Then he lets the curtain fall and follows the body out, and I hear him in the corridor giving orders in a voice that doesn't rise.

Aneb is standing in the middle of the room with his hands out in front of him as if he's still running.

They're shaking. He looks at them like they belong to someone else, and then he puts them behind his back and goes to the bed and stands at the king's shoulder, and after a moment he puts one hand on it. Tut lets him.

"You ran at a knife," I hear myself say, "with nothing in your hands."

"I'm a slave, boy." His voice is low and even, and his jaw is tight enough to crack. "I'm not a wall. He's—" He stops. He looks down at the top of the king's head. "Even pharaohs bleed sand," he says, quieter. "Somebody has to watch the door."

"The Commander watches the door."

"The Commander watches the Vizier's door. I watch this one."

*File that.*

Tut puts his cone down on the board, carefully, on the square he was going to move to. There's blood on the board. There's blood on the square. He sets the cone in it.

"You didn't move," he says to me. "When the man with the knife came. Everyone moves."

"You didn't move either."

"I'm used to it."

I look at the board. The spools have gone under the bed. One of my pieces is lying in the blood on its side.

"That's the third this season," Tut says. "Your turn."

I pick up the spool. I put it where it was. My hand will not stop shaking, and I let it shake, because the king has just told me that everyone moves and he has decided I am the exception, and a boy who doesn't move doesn't shake either, and I would rather he saw the hand than the truth.

"Pharaoh." I keep my voice down where his is. "Who sent him?"

"Someone who loves me very much, probably." He throws the sticks. Three. "They all say they do."

"He froze," Aneb says over the top of his head, to nobody. "The pet. He sat there like a stone and let the man come."

"He didn't move," Tut says, as if it were the same sentence.

It isn't. I know what my body did. Aneb knows what my body did; he's eighteen in an eighteen-year-old's body and he knows exactly what it is to be too slow. The king, who is nine, saw a boy who didn't flinch while his slave flinched for him, and I can see him deciding what it means, and I can see that I'm going to be allowed to let him.

*They see a child. One of them sees a brave one. It's a start.*

The third this season.

I sit on the floor of the only soft room in the palace and I count. The season is sixty days, if I have the calendar right, and I have the calendar right, because my mother drew it on the wall over my bed when I was seven. Three men with knives in sixty days. The old fan-bearer last night, for asking a question. Whoever had my room before me, eleven days.

If this is how the king lives, with his guards on the wall and the Shield of the Living Pharaoh one door away, then what happens to the king's foreign pet, who has no guards, no shield, no name anyone here can say without looking at him sideways, and who sleeps behind a bar that locks from the outside?

I don't know who that man was. I don't know who the Vizier's officers are, or why the High Priest looked at the Vizier before he read the scale, or where the little door behind the curtain goes, or who has a key to it, or what a man with a knife wants when he stops.

Because he stopped. I was a foot away, and I saw it. The point stopped a hand's width from the gold collar, and his eyes went to the door, and then the khopesh came. In that order.

A man who wants a king dead does not stop to look at the door.

I don't know what that means. I know that I don't know, which is the only thing I've learned since the cherry road that's been worth anything.

In the knot of my kilt, against my hip, a grain of gold sand lets go of the glass.

I need someone who knows how this palace works. And I need him before the fourth.
