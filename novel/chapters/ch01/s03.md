**Thebes. Noon.**

The heat is the first thing.

Not warmth. *Heat.* It presses down on the back of my neck like a hand. It comes up off the ground through the soles of my bare feet. The air is so hot and dry that the first breath I take hurts all the way down.

The second thing is the noise.

Shouting. Hundreds of voices, all at once, somewhere close. A donkey braying like a broken hinge. The slap of water against wood. Someone laughing, someone arguing, someone singing three words over and over in a cracked, tuneless voice.

I'm lying in a narrow lane between two walls of mud brick. The walls are the colour of dry bread. A strip of sky above them burns so blue it looks painted.

I sit up.

I'm wearing a blue shirt with red sleeves.

I stare down at it for a long time.

It's the shirt. Not one like it. *The* shirt, from the museum summer, with the small ink stain on the hem where I tested my father's fountain pen and got scolded for it. Below it, a pair of faded shorts. No shoes. No blazer. No phone. No wallet.

My hands are in my lap.

They're small.

I hold them up. Thin wrists. Short, blunt fingers. The calluses from eight years of fencing grips are gone. On my left wrist, a frayed linen cord, the bracelet my mother wove for me, faded from red to the colour of old rust. It used to sit tight on my wrist. Now it hangs loose.

*You will keep what you carried closest to your heart.*

I touch the breast pocket of the shirt. Paper crackles inside. I pull it out.

A ten-thousand-yen note, folded twice.

"……"

I laugh. I don't mean to. It's a short, ugly sound, and it comes out high.

High and bright, like a child's.

I clap my hand over my mouth.

*Breathe. Breathe. Think.*

I stand up. The ground is much closer than it should be. The walls of the lane are much taller. I take a step and my balance is wrong, my centre of gravity too low, my legs too short, as if someone has rebuilt me out of smaller parts and forgotten to tell me.

I put one hand on the wall to steady myself.

Then I walk toward the noise.

***

The lane opens, and the world falls on me.

A market. A huge one. A sea of linen awnings stretched on poles, sagging in the heat, row after row after row of them sloping down a dusty square toward a river.

And the river.

It's brown and enormous and slow, wider than any river I've ever seen. Wooden barges heavy with sacks of grain bump against a stone wharf, and half-naked men carry the sacks up the steps on their shoulders, singing. Beyond the river, a strip of green fields, so bright it hurts. And beyond that, desert. White, glowing, endless desert, and a line of pale cliffs on the horizon.

I turn my head.

Upriver, above the roofs, there's a wall of stone so high and long it looks like a cliff someone built on purpose. Behind it, a needle of stone stabs at the sky, its tip sheathed in something that throws the sun back like a mirror.

I know that needle.

I've seen it in photographs, grey and weathered, in the textbooks on my bedroom floor. I've never seen it with its tip still covered in gold.

An obelisk.

The great temple of Amun. Karnak.

Which means this river is the Nile. And this city is Thebes. *Waset,* my mother corrects, in my head, because she always insisted on the old names. *The city of Amun.*

*In a time long past. Not your past. An older one.*

I stand at the edge of the market of Thebes, three and a half thousand years before I was born, and I count.

*Calm. Count. Find the exits.*

Four streets lead out of the square. The wharf below. Mud-brick buildings on three sides, two storeys at most. A larger building with a guarded gate, stacked with clay-sealed jars: a granary. The seals on the jars are stamped with a ram's head.

Amun's ram. Temple grain.

My father's voice, very dry: *Whoever owns the grain owns Egypt.*

The heat is making me dizzy. My stomach makes a noise. A long, painful, hollow noise.

When did I last eat?

Yesterday's dinner, eighteen years old, in my rented room, cold convenience-store rice. But this body didn't eat that. This body hasn't eaten anything, ever, as far as it knows. And it is starving.

***

I let the crowd carry me in.

Everyone is taller than me. That's the first thing I learn about being ten again: the world is made of hips and elbows. I push between sweating bodies in white linen kilts, women in straight sheath dresses with braided black hair, a man with a shaved head carrying two geese by their necks. A boy my size runs past with a basket of onions and doesn't look at me twice.

Nobody looks at me twice.

Not yet.

The shouting is words now. I didn't notice when it changed. One moment it was noise, and the next it's a fishmonger yelling that his perch were swimming this morning, and a woman arguing about the weight of a copper ring, and a beer seller swearing by Hathor that his jars aren't watered.

I understand every word.

Each one arrives a half second late, like a film with the subtitles out of sync. And each one feels wrong in my head, heavy and strange.

*The words will sit in your mouth like a borrowed tooth.*

The smell of bread finds me before I see it.

It comes from a stall under a stained linen awning, where a big woman with flour on her forearms is pulling flat loaves out of a clay oven. Emmer bread, coarse and brown, with ash on the bottom. Beside her, on the counter, sits a wide jar of glazed blue clay, cracked down one side and full to the brim with water.

I stop in front of the counter. It comes up to my chest.

I look down into the jar.

A face looks back at me.

A child's face. Round-cheeked. Small chin. A pale, soft, foreign face that has never seen this much sun.

The hair is blond.

Not light brown. Not dyed. Gold-blond, the colour of the wheat stalks in a museum painting, falling in sharp, untidy layers across the forehead.

The eyes are blue.

My eyes were brown. My whole life, my eyes were brown.

There's only one thing in that face I recognise. A thin white scar, cutting down through the left eyebrow.

My scar. On a stranger.

I jerk back from the jar so fast I nearly fall. My heart is going like a fist on a door.

*They gave me a different face.*

*No. They gave me a different everything, and left me the scar so I'd know it was still me.*

"You going to buy something, or just stare at my water?"

The bread woman is looking down at me with her floury arms folded.

I open my mouth. What comes out is a child's voice, high and clear, speaking her language with an accent I can hear even through my own ears.

"One loaf, please."

She looks at me differently. The way you look at a dog that just said a word.

"One loaf," she repeats. "With what?"

*Barter,* I think. *No coins yet. Grain, linen, copper by weight. Deben.*

I don't have grain, or linen, or copper.

I have a ten-thousand-yen note.

It's stupid. I know it's stupid even as I do it. But I'm so hungry that my hands are shaking, and some animal part of me that is very, very ten years old thinks: *money is money.*

I unfold the bill and hold it up.

***

The woman takes it between two fingers.

She frowns. She turns it over. She holds it up to the light, and the watermark shows through, a ghostly face floating in the paper.

She drops it on the counter like it bit her.

"What is this?"

"It's money. Where I come from."

"Where is that?"

I don't answer.

She picks it up again, more carefully. "Who's this?" She taps the printed face of a stern old man in a high Western collar. "Is this a king? Whose king has a face like that?" She squints at the numbers and the strange curling letters. "What does it say? Is it a spell?"

"No. It's just—"

"Is it a *curse*?"

A man at the next stall leans over to look. Then another. A woman with a basket of figs. Two porters from the wharf, dripping river water.

The crowd closes in, the way crowds do, all at once.

"That's not papyrus."

"Look at the colours on it."

"Look at *him*. Look at his hair."

"Where's your mother, boy?"

"*Khat-su,*" someone says. Sand-foreign. It sounds like spitting.

Somebody else says, "Spy."

The word goes through the crowd like a match through dry grass.

*Spy. Spy for who? Spy for Hatti. They send children now. Look at him, he's not even brown. Call the Medjay.*

"I'm not a spy," I say, and my high voice cracks, and nobody hears it.

Over the heads of the crowd, two long wooden staffs are coming toward us. The men holding them have the lean, hard look of police everywhere: dark-skinned, bare-chested, with leather bands on their wrists and no patience in their faces.

Medjay. City police.

The bread woman holds up my ten-thousand-yen note to them like evidence.

*Of course,* I think. *Just another variable I didn't account for.*

I snatch the bill out of her floury fingers.

And I run.

***

*Small,* I think, as I duck between the fig woman's legs. *I'm small. Use it.*

I go low through the crowd, under baskets, between knees, past a startled donkey's belly. Behind me, a staff cracks against somebody's shoulder and a man yells. The Medjay are shouting for people to move. People don't move fast enough. People never do.

*Breathe. You're small, not stupid. Small hides. Stupid dies.*

Ahead of me, a two-wheeled cart sits tipped on its side by the wharf steps, its axle snapped in half. A man is kneeling beside it, cursing at the broken wood.

I hear a Medjay right behind me. His sandals slap the dirt. He's closing, because his legs are twice as long as mine.

I veer toward the cart. Grab the loose end of the broken axle as I pass. Yank it.

It swings out sideways, a long bar of wood, at exactly the height of a grown man's shins.

There's a crack, and a howl, and the slap of a body hitting the ground.

*Fencing,* I think wildly. *It's just distance and timing. It's just—*

The second Medjay comes around the cart from the other side.

There's nowhere left to go except the edge of the wharf.

Below the edge is a stone wall, as tall as two men. Below the wall is a strip of black river mud, and then the Nile.

I don't think about it. If I think about it, I'll stop.

I jump.

For one long second there's nothing under me. Then the mud hits me like a door. It's cold and stinking and deep, and I go in to my knees. Pain shoots up both legs. I fall forward onto my hands. The smell of fish and rot is so thick I gag.

I look up.

There's a soldier standing in the mud in front of me.

Not Medjay. A real soldier. A tall young man with a leather cap and a cowhide shield painted with black and white patches, and a spear in his hand, and an expression of mild, tired surprise, as if small blond boys fall out of the sky in front of him every afternoon.

My hand goes to my hip.

There's no sword there. There's barely a hip there. It's a reflex from a fencing strip in another body, and it's useless.

I try anyway. I drop my weight back, the way I've done ten thousand times on a fencing strip, and feint left and go right.

My feet are stuck in the mud.

The butt of the spear takes me in the stomach.

It isn't even a hard blow. I can tell that it isn't. He barely swings. But this body weighs nothing, and it folds around the wood like wet paper, and every bit of air I have leaves me at once.

I'm on my face in the mud.

I can't breathe. I can't see. My whole world is the taste of river and the noise my own lungs are making, trying to remember how lungs work.

A hand grabs the back of my shirt and lifts me out of the mud like a kitten.

"Got him," the soldier calls up the wall. He sounds bored.

***

They tie my wrists with rope. Rough palm fibre that bites the skin.

Then they march me back up through the market.

The crowd parts for the soldier and the Medjay the way water parts for a boat. People stare at me. Children point. An old woman makes a sign against evil with two fingers.

I keep walking.

And because I can't do anything else, I watch.

I watch who bows, and to whom. The Medjay nod to the soldier, not the other way around. Everyone steps aside for a man in long, spotless white linen with a shaved head and a leopard skin over one shoulder. A priest. The soldier lowers his eyes to him too.

At the granary gate, a scribe sits cross-legged with a palette on his knees, counting sacks as they go in. Every sack is stamped with the ram of Amun. Next to him stands a guard who isn't a soldier and isn't Medjay. He wears the same white as the priest.

The temple guards its own grain. With its own men.

*Whoever owns the grain owns Egypt.*

*So who owns the grain, Father?*

I count my steps. Two hundred and forty from the wharf to the edge of the market. I map the turns. I note the gate, the guards, the scribe's ink pot, the cracked blue jar. I note that nobody in this entire market looks at the soldier's face when he passes, and nobody looks at the priest at all.

They see a child, dragged through the street on a rope.

They don't see me counting.

At the end of the market, there's another wharf, and a long, low boat with a reed canopy, and rowers waiting.

The soldier lifts me in like a sack of grain.

We push off. The city slides away behind us. The brown water is wide and slow and full of light. Ahead of us, on the far bank, past the green fields, stands a long white palace with painted columns, at the edge of the desert, on the side of the river where the Egyptians bury their dead.

I'm wet and filthy and shaking, and I can't feel my hands.

But I'm still holding the bill.

Nobody thought to take it from me. My fist closed around it in the mud and hasn't opened since.

A face no one here can read. A number no one here can count.

The only proof, in this whole world, that I'm not insane.

I hold on to it all the way across the river.
