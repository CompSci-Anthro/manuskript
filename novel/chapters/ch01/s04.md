**The palace. Afternoon.**

The cell is a hole.

That's all it is. A square pit cut into the rock under some back corner of the palace, with a wooden grate over the top and a ladder that they pull up after them.

The soldier doesn't use the ladder for me. He lowers me by the rope on my wrists until my toes are a body's length off the floor, and then he lets go.

I land on my side on cold stone. Something in my shoulder makes a noise I don't like.

The grate slams shut above me. A bar slides across it.

Footsteps leave.

I lie there in the dark and breathe, and listen to my own heartbeat, and smell river mud drying on my skin.

*Get up,* I tell myself.

I don't.

I lie on the floor of a pit in ancient Egypt, ten years old, tied like an animal, and for one long moment, I let myself feel all of it.

The tree. The weight. The broom stopping.

Soren's message, still unanswered. It will be unanswered forever now.

Six books in brown paper at Mrs. Hayashi's shop that no one will ever come to collect.

*I wish I was ten again.*

"……"

I press my forehead against the stone until it hurts.

Then I get up.

***

"That was a long fall, for someone so small."

I freeze.

There's someone else in the pit with me.

As my eyes adjust, I see him: a man sitting against the far wall with his knees drawn up. Middle-aged, heavy around the middle, with a good linen kilt gone grey with dirt. His wig is gone. Without it, his shaved head looks naked and soft. His wrists are tied like mine.

"I didn't see you," I say.

My voice comes out formal, the way it does when I'm frightened. Each word placed carefully, like stepping stones.

The man's eyebrows go up. "You speak like an old scribe."

"I was taught by old people."

He laughs. It's a tired, kind laugh. "What did you do, little scribe?"

"I tried to buy bread."

"And what did you pay with?"

"The wrong thing."

He laughs again, harder, and then stops, and wipes his eyes with his tied hands.

"And you?" I ask.

He's quiet for a while.

"I am the overseer of granaries for the western district," he says. "Was. This spring, a decree came down in the king's name. My district's grain is to go to the house of Amun, to rebuild the god's temples. All of it. Even the seed for next year." He looks at his hands. "My workers have children. So I went to court, and I knelt, and I asked the king if he had truly ordered such a thing."

"That's all?"

"That's all." He smiles at me in the dark. "It turns out that is a great deal."

*He asked whether the king had really given the order,* I think. *Not whether the order was fair. Whether the king gave it.*

*Which means he thinks someone else did.*

My father would have liked this man. My father always said the most dangerous question in any court is *who actually decided this?*

"What will they do to you?" I ask.

"They'll weigh my heart," the overseer says simply. "And they will find it heavy. And afterward, someone will go to my tomb with a chisel and cut my name off the walls, so the gods won't know where to find me."

He says it the way you'd talk about rain.

"That is the real death, you know," he adds. "Not the knife. Being forgotten."

*Don't let them forget his name.*

I sit down beside him in the dark. Neither of us speaks again until the grate opens.

***

They take us up together.

Through a kitchen court that smells of smoke and goose fat. Through a long colonnade where servants flatten themselves against the wall to let us pass. Through a pair of cedar doors as tall as a house.

And into the throne room.

It runs away from me like a forest made of stone. Two rows of columns as thick as tree trunks march toward the far end, each one painted to look like a bundle of lotus stalks, blue and green and gold, with open flowers at the top. Above them, the ceiling glitters with gold leaf. High up in the walls, narrow windows let in long, slanted bars of sunlight, and through those bars drifts smoke, sweet and heavy, curling up from bronze lamps on tall stands.

Myrrh. The smell is so thick I can taste it.

Men stand in the shadows between the columns. Dozens of them. White linen, gold collars, heavy black wigs. They don't talk. They barely move. They stand like statues in a tomb, and they all turn their heads to watch us walk in.

The floor is painted.

That's what I'll remember later. Not the gold. The floor. Someone has covered the whole floor in plaster and painted it like a marsh: blue water, green reeds, fish swimming, ducks bursting up from the papyrus with their wings spread. It's so beautiful that for one second I forget to be afraid.

Then a hand on the back of my neck shoves me down onto it.

"Kiss the ground before the Lord of the Two Lands."

My face hits a painted fish.

***

I keep my forehead down. But I can see sideways, along the floor, between the columns. And I can listen.

Somewhere ahead of me, a staff strikes stone three times.

A herald's voice announces a list of titles so long I lose count. *Strong Bull. Beloved of Amun. Lord of the Two Lands. Lord of Appearances.* And a name at the end of it.

I hear the name and stop breathing.

*Tutankhamun.*

I don't look up. If I look up now, they'll hit me. But my whole body goes cold, then hot, then cold again.

My father in the museum. His hand on my shoulder. A gold mask behind glass, with eyes painted in black and white and a face so young it looked like a doll's.

*King at nine years old, Kael. Think about that.*

*Dead at nineteen.*

They bring the overseer forward first.

He goes down on his knees beside me. I can see his tied hands on the painted water, and the way they're shaking now, even though his voice in the pit was steady.

A new voice speaks.

It's soft. That's the first thing about it. Soft and deep and smooth, polite as a host welcoming guests, and it doesn't need to be loud, because the whole room goes silent the moment it begins.

"The overseer of the western granaries came before this court and asked whether the word of the king was truly the king's word." A pause, long and unhurried. "He has questioned the Lord of the Two Lands, in his own hall, before the gods. There is no greater disorder than that."

Another pause.

"Order before mercy. Let his heart be weighed."

I turn my head a fraction, just enough to see.

The voice belongs to a man standing at the foot of the dais, at the right hand of the throne.

He is very tall and very thin, so thin his robe hangs from his shoulders like it's hanging from a doorframe. The robe is deep indigo, embroidered with tiny golden figures that I know are hieroglyphs for Amun. His head is shaved and gleams in the lamplight, with a single ornament of gold above his brow. His face is narrow and calm, and there are thin raised scars on his cheekbones, too regular to be from any accident. A wide collar of blue lapis and red carnelian lies across his chest. In one hand, he holds a tall staff topped with a falcon's head.

His fingers are wrapped in strips of linen, like old bandages. Over the linen, he wears rings. Many rings. Gold, heavy, carved.

*The Vizier,* someone whispers behind me. *Amenhotep.*

*Amenhotep?*

I search my memory, the shelves and shelves of it. My parents' books. My mother's handwriting on the chart above my bed.

I don't remember a vizier named Amenhotep. Not under this king.

*Either my father's books were wrong,* I think, *or I am.*

The Vizier's eyes move across the room. They're light brown, almost gold in the lamplight, and they don't stop on anyone for long.

They pass over me.

They don't stop.

***

A priest comes forward with a set of scales.

He's young, with a shaved head and a leopard skin, and he carries a small bronze balance on a stand, the kind I've seen painted on the walls of a hundred tombs. He sets it down on the painted floor in front of the overseer.

On one pan, he lays a single white feather.

On the other, he lays a little amulet of red stone, carved in the shape of a heart.

My stomach turns over.

I know this scene. Everyone who has opened a book on Egypt knows this scene. The Weighing of the Heart. A dead man's heart, weighed against the feather of Ma'at, of truth. If the heart is heavier than the feather, a monster with a crocodile's head devours it, and the soul dies forever.

But that happens after death. In the underworld. In front of Osiris.

Not in a throne room, in the afternoon, in front of a living man.

*This wasn't in any of the books,* I think. *The books only had the paintings on the tomb walls. Nobody wrote down what the living did with them.*

The priest lifts the balance by its handle. The pans swing.

I watch his hand.

I've always watched hands. Fencing teaches you that. The blade lies, the eyes lie, but the hand tells you where the attack is going a split second before it goes.

The priest's thumb slides down the bronze arm and rests, very lightly, on the pan with the heart.

The heart sinks.

"It is heavy," the priest announces. "The heart is heavy with disorder."

A murmur through the columns. Nobody sounds surprised.

"Ammit take it," someone says, and others repeat it, low, like a prayer.

"No," the overseer says. "No, Majesty, please, I only asked—"

Two guards lift him by the arms.

He doesn't fight. That's the worst part. He's a soft, heavy, middle-aged man who counts grain for a living, and he knows exactly how this ends, so he doesn't fight. He just looks back over his shoulder toward the throne, the whole way, as they drag him between the columns and into the shadow.

I hear him say, very quietly, "My name is—"

A wet, heavy *thunk.*

Then silence.

A servant girl hurries out from between the columns with a bucket and a cloth. She kneels down where the shadow ends and begins to scrub at something on the painted floor.

The scrubbing is the only sound in the hall.

I realise I'm shaking. I put my tied hands flat on the floor to stop it.

*He asked who really gave the order,* I think. *And they killed him for it. In front of the king. So the king could watch.*

The Vizier hasn't moved. He stands at the foot of the dais with his staff, perfectly calm, as if he has just watched someone pour wine.

***

"The foreign boy," the herald calls.

The hand on my neck hauls me upright, onto my knees.

The soldier from the wharf steps forward and bows to a man standing beside the throne. "Commander Nakhtmin. A foreign child, taken in the market at the river. He paid for bread with this."

He grabs my wrists. He pries my fist open, finger by finger.

I don't let go easily. It doesn't matter. He's an adult, and I'm not, and in the end the bill comes free, crumpled and stained with mud.

He holds it up.

The court leans forward as one.

"What is it?"

"A spell. Look at the face on it."

"It's no writing I have ever seen. A cipher. A Hittite cipher."

"Look at the boy. Look at his *hair.*"

"Sent by Hatti to spy on the king."

"Sent to curse him."

"A child," someone says, doubtfully.

"A child is the perfect spy. Who looks at a child?"

"Majesty." A fat official with a heavy gold collar steps forward and bows low. "The law is clear. A foreigner taken in the city, with sorcery in his hand. Let his heart be weighed."

"Let his heart be weighed," others echo.

The young priest picks up his scales again.

The feather on one side. The red stone heart on the other.

He doesn't even bother to reset them.

*Don't panic.* My heart is hammering so hard I can see my vision pulse. *Think. They see a child. What does a child get that a man doesn't? Pity? There's no pity in this room. Mercy? He just watched a man die for asking a question.*

"I am no spy," I say.

My voice rings out, high and bright, in that perfectly silent hall.

And every word is wrong. The words are too careful, too old, too formal for the face they're coming out of. I can hear it as I speak, the way the court hears it: a little boy talking like a priest who's a hundred years old.

"I am a traveller from very far away. I was robbed and left in the city with nothing. That paper is all I have. I meant no harm to the king, and I would never—"

The hand on my neck slaps me across the back of the head, hard enough that I see white.

"You do not speak in the presence of the king unless he bids you speak."

The court murmurs. Not angry. Uneasy. A few of them step back from me, and one makes the sign against evil.

*Good job, Kael. You've just proved you're a demon.*

I lift my head anyway.

For the first time since they dragged me in, I look up the steps of the dais. Past the Vizier and his indigo robes. Past a broad-shouldered young man standing beside the throne like a wall, with a curved bronze sword at his hip and a scar along his jaw and dark eyes that don't blink.

To the throne.

It's gold. All of it. Gold leaf over carved wood, with lion heads on the arms and lion feet on the legs, and wings of inlaid blue and red stone wrapped around the back. It's beautiful, and it's enormous. It was built for a grown man. For a god.

The boy sitting in it doesn't fill half of it.

He's nine years old.

He's small and fine-boned and very still, with brown skin and a narrow face, wearing a striped headdress of blue and gold that falls to his shoulders, with a golden cobra rearing at his forehead. A wide golden collar inlaid with lapis covers his thin chest like armour. Someone has tied a false beard to his chin, a long braided thing of blue and gold, and it looks heavy and wrong on a face that young.

His feet don't reach the footstool.

They dangle above it, in little gold sandals. And the footstool beneath them is painted with figures of foreign prisoners, men with their arms tied behind their backs, lying face down, so that the king can rest his feet on his enemies.

Bound foreigners.

Like me.

The boy isn't looking at the court. He isn't looking at the priest or the scales. His dark eyes have gone to the Vizier, and stayed there, waiting, the way a child in class watches the teacher to see what the answer is supposed to be.

His fingers are wrapped tight around a black stone scarab pinned to his collar.

*He's not in control here,* I think. *He's not in control of anything.*

*He's the only person in this room who's as trapped as I am.*

The priest lifts his scales.

And the king of Egypt turns his head, and looks down the steps, and finds me looking back.

Our eyes lock.

His are very dark, very tired, much older than nine. For a long moment, neither of us moves.

Then something in his face changes.

It's small. A loosening around the eyes. The look of someone who has been alone in a crowded room for a very long time, and has just noticed that someone else is too.

His fingers let go of the scarab.
