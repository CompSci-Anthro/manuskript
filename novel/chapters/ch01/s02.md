**Nowhere. No time.**

I wake up because something is counting.

*Tick.*

Not a clock. Softer. Like a single grain of sand landing on glass.

*Tick.*

I open my eyes.

The sky above me is black. Not night-black. There are no stars, no moon, no clouds. It's the black of a closed eye.

And yet I can see.

I'm lying on my back in sand. Grey sand, fine as ash, cold under my palms. I sit up slowly, waiting for the pain of a crushed spine, broken ribs, anything.

Nothing hurts.

That frightens me more than pain would.

*Tick.*

I get to my feet.

The desert runs away in every direction, flat and grey and endless. And standing in it, in rows that stretch to the horizon, are sundials.

Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Stone pillars as tall as my waist, each one topped with a round dial and a slanted blade of bronze. Some are carved with numbers I know. Some with symbols I half know: Roman numerals, Chinese characters, Egyptian glyphs, marks that might be older than writing.

There's no sun.

Every dial casts a shadow anyway.

And every shadow points at me.

"……"

I turn around. They turn too. Ten thousand thin black lines across the grey sand, all bending to follow me, like the needles of compasses that have found north.

*Breathe,* I tell myself. *Breathe. Think.*

The tree fell. I remember the weight. I remember the sound of the broom stopping.

So either this is a dream, or I'm in a hospital on very good drugs, or—

"You are dead, Kael."

The voice comes from behind me.

It isn't loud. It's dry and quiet, like pages turning in an empty library. But it lands in my bones, and every grain of sand in the desert trembles with it.

I turn around.

***

There's someone sitting on the nearest sundial.

At least, I think it's someone. When I look straight at it, it's a man, tall and thin, wrapped in something pale. When I look at its face, the face isn't there. It's made of dust, the gold kind you see floating in a sunbeam through a dusty window, and the dust never settles long enough to become eyes or a mouth.

It's old. I don't know how I know. The way you know a mountain is old.

"You are dead," it says again, patiently, as if I'm a slow student. "The tree was older than you. It fell. That is all."

My mouth is dry. "Who are you?"

The dust shifts. It might be a smile.

"Your people have had many names for me. Most of them have forgotten all of them. I am the one who keeps what is gone." A hand made of drifting gold gestures at the sundials. "The past. I keep the past."

"The God of the Past," I say, and my own voice sounds thin and stupid in all that silence.

"If you like."

I should be screaming. I know that. Some part of me is screaming, a long way down, in the dark. But the part of me on top is the part that sat through three years of police interviews and funeral arrangements and paperwork with my parents' names on it, and that part has learned to go cold when things get bad.

So I go cold.

"If I'm dead," I say, "why am I talking to you?"

"Because you asked me for something."

"I didn't ask you for anything."

"You stood on my door and made a wish."

The shrine. The sealed stone shrine under the oldest tree, with the worn mark of a circle and a single line. Like a sundial with only its shadow left.

*Oh.*

"I wasn't talking to you," I say. "I wasn't talking to anyone."

"Everyone who speaks to the past is speaking to me." The dust leans forward. "*I wish I was ten again. Life was so much simpler.* Those were your words. I heard them. I have decided to grant them."

"No." It comes out too fast. "No. That's not what I meant. That's just something people *say*—"

"Is it?"

The god holds up one hand. In it, there's an hourglass.

It's small. Old glass, clouded and scratched, held in a frame of dark wood. The sand inside is the same dusty gold as the god's face.

"You will be a child again," it says. "In a time long past. Not your past. An older one."

It turns the hourglass over.

The sand begins to fall.

*Tick.*

So that was the sound.

"You have two years," the god says. "Two floods of the great river. When the second flood goes down, the past lets go of you, and you return to where you left."

"Where I left," I repeat.

Under the tree. Under the weight. On the wet pavement, with my phone buzzing in my pocket and Mrs. Hayashi's broom standing still.

"You're sending me back to die."

"You are already dead," the god says, gently. "I am lending you two years. What you do with them is yours."

"And if I don't want them?"

The god doesn't answer that.

The sand keeps falling.

***

"Rules," I say. My hands are shaking. I fold them under my arms so it can't see. "If you're doing this, there have to be rules. Where am I going? How do I survive there? How do I get back? Can I get back?"

"You ask many questions."

"My parents were historians. Questions are all I have."

For the first time, the dust goes still.

"Yes," the god says softly. "I know who your parents were."

The cold in my chest turns to something sharper.

"What does that mean?"

"You will understand their tongue," the god says, as if I hadn't spoken, "and they will understand yours. The words will sit in your mouth like a borrowed tooth. They will hear that you are not one of them."

"What does that *mean*? What do you know about my parents?"

"You will keep what you carried closest to your heart. Nothing more."

"Answer me!"

The sundials flinch. Ten thousand shadows flicker. I have never shouted at anything in my life, and I've just shouted at a god.

The god looks at me with its face of falling dust for a long, long moment.

Then it says, in my mother's voice:

*"Don't let them forget his name."*

Every hair on my body stands up.

"Stop it."

"She understood," the god says, in its own voice again. "Not all of it. Enough."

"Understood *what*? Whose name? Why me? Why would you—"

The desert begins to blow away.

It starts at the horizon. The farthest sundials crumble into grains and lift into the black sky like smoke, and the wind carries them toward me, and there are pictures in the sand.

A river, brown and enormous, wider than any river should be.

A boy in a gold collar, sitting on a throne much too big for him.

Lamplight running along the edge of a blade.

A stone wall carved with a name inside an oval ring, and a chisel striking it, and striking it, and striking it, until the name is gone.

"Where are you sending me?" I shout over the wind. "*Why?*"

The god only smiles. I can't see its mouth, but I know it's smiling.

"Simple?" it says. "We shall see."

The sand hits me like a wave.

It fills my eyes, my mouth, my lungs. I'm falling. I'm shrinking. My bones fold in on themselves like a telescope closing, and my blazer turns heavy and huge around me and then isn't there at all, and the last thing I hear is that sound, that small, patient sound of time running out—

*Tick.*

—and then sunlight.

White, blinding, furious sunlight.

And the smell of fish.
