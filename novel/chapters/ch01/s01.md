The Nile smells of fish, myrrh and rot, and in the market of Thebes I am ten years old again, with eighteen years of guilt behind my eyes.

But that comes later.

First, I have to die.

**Japan. Spring. 7:42 a.m.**

My parents were obsessed with history.

Other children got picture books about trains. I got a laminated chart of the Eighteenth Dynasty, taped to the wall above my bed, with the pharaohs' names in my mother's neat handwriting. Other families went to the beach on holidays. We went to museums, and my father stood in front of glass cases for so long that the guards started to watch him.

He would point at a cracked jar, or a broken statue with no nose, and say, "Kael. Somebody made this. Somebody's hands touched this, three thousand years ago. Think about that."

I thought about it.

I thought about it so much that I can still recite the kings of Egypt faster than I can recite my own phone number.

Three years ago, someone set fire to our house. My parents were inside. The police said arson, and then they said the case was open, and then they stopped saying anything at all.

The books survived. Most of them. They live in boxes in my rented room now, and they still smell of smoke.

So do I, some mornings.

***

The cherry road is the long way to school.

Nobody takes it. It adds nine minutes. I know because I counted, the first week after the fire, when I needed something to count.

I take it every day.

The old trees lean over the lane from both sides, so the road becomes a tunnel of pink. Last night's rain still shines on the pavement. Petals stick to it like wet paper. At the bottom of the slope, the grey concrete of the school gate waits in cold shadow, the way it always does.

A vending machine hums beside the bus shelter.

My phone buzzes in my blazer pocket.

I don't take it out.

I already know what's on it. The homeroom group chat counting down the days to the entrance exams. A reminder from the university my parents taught at, cheerfully confirming my application to the history department. Two messages from the fencing club captain asking whether I'm *really* quitting.

And one from Soren.

My twin brother lives with our uncle in Osaka now. He writes to me every month, and every month I read the first line in the preview and leave it there.

This month's first line says: *It's been three years. Are you coming on Sunday or not?*

Not.

I walk.

Thirty-one steps to the first lamppost. Twenty-eight to the second. I keep my eyes on the pavement and my breathing slow.

My reflection slides past in a puddle. Black hair that needs cutting. Black school uniform. A pale face that people say looks tired, and a thin white scar through my left eyebrow, where a training foil snapped at a tournament when I was twelve. It missed my eye by the width of a coin.

I looked at that scar in the mirror every day for a month afterward, feeling lucky.

I don't remember the last time I felt lucky.

***

"Kael. Good morning."

Mrs. Hayashi is sweeping in front of her tea shop, the way she does every dawn. She is very old and very small, and she sweeps petals with the seriousness of a priest.

"Good morning, Mrs. Hayashi."

"You look terrible."

"Thank you. I studied late."

She makes a noise that tells me exactly what she thinks of studying late. Then she leans on her broom and studies me instead.

Mrs. Hayashi read old texts with my parents, back when I was too young to care what the texts said. She is the only adult in this town who still talks to me as if I'm a person and not a tragedy.

"Your mother's order came," she says.

I stop.

"Her order."

"Six books. From a dealer in Kyoto. She ordered them before…" Mrs. Hayashi's mouth tightens. "Before. They only found them this winter. I've been keeping them for you."

Six books my mother wanted. Six books she never got to open.

My hand moves on its own. I take out my wallet and pull out the only bill inside, a ten-thousand-yen note I'd been saving for something I can't remember now.

"How much do I owe you?"

She looks at the bill. She looks at me.

"Put that away. After school. I'll wrap them properly."

"I can pay now."

"After school," she says again, in a voice that ends the conversation. "Go. You'll be late."

I fold the bill twice and slide it into the breast pocket of my blazer, over my heart, where I won't forget it.

Then I bow, and keep walking.

Behind me, the broom starts again. *Scritch. Scritch.*

***

Halfway down the slope stands the oldest tree.

It's twice as thick as the others, and so crooked it looks like it's bowing. Its roots have split around something at its base: a small stone shrine, no taller than my knee, its little door sealed with old grey mortar.

There's a mark carved over the door. A circle with a single line through it, like the face of a sundial with only its shadow left. The stone is worn so smooth that the mark is almost gone.

I've walked past it every day for three years. I don't know which god it belongs to. I've never asked.

Every morning, Mrs. Hayashi sweeps the shrine clean. Every morning, the old tree drops the same low branch across the pavement, as if it's doing it on purpose.

It's there now. I step over it.

On the other side of the lane, a group of elementary school kids are racing to the corner. Their yellow caps bob through the petals. One of them, a boy of nine or ten, is trying to catch the falling blossoms in his mouth.

He's wearing a blue shirt with red sleeves.

I had a shirt exactly like that. My mother bought it for a museum trip, the summer I turned ten. I wore it until the sleeves stopped at my elbows.

The boy misses a petal, trips, and lands on his backside on the wet pavement. His friends howl with laughter. He laughs too, louder than any of them, with his whole body.

I can't remember the last time I laughed like that.

That summer, I think. The museum. My father's hand on my shoulder in front of a gold mask, telling me the boy who wore it had been king at nine years old.

*Think about that, Kael.*

The kids run around the corner. Their voices fade.

The road is quiet.

My phone buzzes again. The pressure behind my eyes builds, the way it does every morning now, a weight that never quite lifts. Exams. Applications. A future my parents would have been proud of, picked out for me by everyone except me.

A brother I don't answer.

An empty room full of burned books.

My mother's voice, in the smoke.

I don't usually let myself remember that part. The fire, the heat, my father's shape in the doorway and then not. My mother on the grass outside, her hand gripping my sleeve, her eyes wide and clear. Her last words weren't "I love you." They weren't my name.

They were: *Don't let them forget his name.*

I still don't know whose name she meant.

I stand under the oldest tree and breathe, and a petal lands on my sleeve, and something in my chest that has been holding very still for three years finally gives way.

"I wish I was ten again," I say to no one. "Life was so much simpler."

The words hang in the cold air.

The branches over my head stop moving.

Every one of them, all at once, as if the whole tree is holding its breath. The petals in the air stop falling. They hang there, perfectly still, a pink snowfall frozen in place.

*That's not—*

**CRACK.**

It's as loud as a gunshot. Louder. It comes from under my feet.

The shrine's grey mortar splits down the middle. The roots heave. The pavement buckles, and the oldest tree on the cherry road tears itself open from the ground up, and leans, and keeps leaning.

Toward me.

Petals explode into the air in a storm of pink.

I see it coming. I have time to see it coming. Every fencing coach I ever had said I was fast, that I read an attack before it started.

My body doesn't move.

*Of course,* I think. *Just another variable I didn't account—*

The trunk comes down.

There's no pain. Just weight, enormous and final, and the smell of wet bark and spring rain, and somewhere very far away, the sound of a broom stopping.

My phone is still buzzing in my pocket.

Then it isn't.

Then nothing is.
