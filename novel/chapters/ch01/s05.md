The king lifts one small hand.

That's all. He doesn't stand. He doesn't speak. He just raises his hand from the arm of the golden throne, palm out, the way a child in a classroom asks permission to talk.

The priest freezes with the scales in the air.

The whole hall freezes with him.

I can feel it, the way you feel a change in air pressure before a storm. Dozens of grown men in gold collars, all of them suddenly unsure what they're supposed to do. They look at the raised hand. Then, one by one, I watch their eyes slide sideways to the foot of the dais.

To the Vizier.

Amenhotep doesn't move. He doesn't look up at the king. He stands with his staff and his rings and his calm, narrow face, and waits, as if he's curious what will happen next.

"Wait," the king says.

His voice is high and thin. It shouldn't carry in a hall that size. It carries anyway.

The fat official in the heavy collar bows so low his wig nearly brushes the floor. "Majesty. Lord of the Two Lands. The law is clear. A foreigner, with sorcery in his hand—"

"The law says the king sentences."

Five words. Flat. No anger in them. Like a child reciting a lesson he's been made to learn by heart.

"Bring him."

***

The hand on my neck hauls me to my feet and marches me forward, over the painted marsh, past the place where the servant girl is still scrubbing, until I'm at the bottom of the dais steps.

Close up, the boy is even smaller.

His wrists are thin as sticks under the gold bracelets. The false beard is tied on with a cord that's rubbed a red line under his jaw. His eyes are lined with black kohl, and under the kohl, there are shadows. He looks like he hasn't slept properly in a long time.

He looks at me.

I look back.

I shouldn't. Every instinct I have from three years of police stations and principals' offices tells me to lower my eyes, to make myself small and harmless and sorry. But I'm already small. And I'm tired of being sorry.

So I look back.

The king of Egypt studies me for a long moment. My blond hair, stiff with river mud. The strange blue shirt. The rope on my wrists. The scar through my eyebrow.

Then he says, quite clearly, so that the whole hall can hear:

"I shall sentence you."

The priest lowers the scales a little.

"By my royal authority," the king says, "I declare you to be my royal playmate and pet."

***

For a second, nobody understands.

I don't understand. The words arrive in my head a half beat late, the way they all do here, and I have to hear them twice.

*Playmate.*

*Pet.*

Then the hall breaks.

It isn't loud. Courtiers don't shout. But the murmur rolls down the columns like a wave, and it's full of things that aren't quite words: shock, disgust, a few nervous laughs smothered behind hands.

The fat official straightens up, red-faced. "Majesty, with respect, the boy is a *foreigner.* He may be a spy, a sorcerer. He should be weighed, not—not *kept*—"

"He belongs to the king now."

"But the law—"

"The law says the king sentences."

"Majesty, surely the Vizier would counsel—"

"Pharaoh has sentenced."

The boy says it louder this time. Not much. But his voice cracks right down the middle of the word *Pharaoh*, and his small hand comes back to the black scarab on his collar and closes around it so tightly his knuckles go pale.

He's terrified.

He's nine years old, and he's terrified, and he's doing it anyway.

*He found the gap,* I think, and something in my chest goes very still. *The heart has to be weighed before the king. So he took the judgment for himself before they could finish. And the sentence he chose is one nobody can carry out with a knife.*

*You can't execute the king's toy. It's his property.*

*That's not a child's trick. That's a lawyer's.*

The silence after *Pharaoh has sentenced* goes on, and on.

Every face in the hall turns, very slowly, to the Vizier.

Amenhotep lets the silence stretch a little longer. Long enough that everyone feels it. Long enough that the king's hand tightens on his scarab one more time.

Then the Vizier bows.

It's a beautiful bow. Smooth and deep, his staff dipping, his rings flashing in the lamplight.

"As the Lord of the Two Lands wills," he says softly.

He straightens.

And for the first time, he looks at me.

It isn't a long look. A second. Less. His light, almost golden eyes rest on my face, and then on my eyes, and then they narrow, very slightly, the way a man narrows his eyes to read small writing.

He says nothing to me. Not a word.

He doesn't need to.

I've been looked at by a lot of adults. Teachers, police officers, social workers, the uncle in Osaka who wanted to take me in and didn't really want to take me in. I know the difference between a look that sees a child and a look that sees *me.*

This one sees me.

*Marked,* I think. *I've just been marked.*

Amenhotep turns to the scribe sitting cross-legged at the foot of the dais and holds out a ringed hand. The soldier from the wharf gives the scribe my muddy bill, and the scribe gives it to the Vizier.

He turns it over once in his linen-wrapped fingers. Looks at the stern old face. Looks at the numbers.

"Record it," he says, and hands it back to the scribe. "Keep it with the court's records. Curious things should be kept somewhere safe."

The scribe bows and tucks my bill into a cedar box.

I watch it go. The last piece of my world, shut in a box in a palace three and a half thousand years from home.

"Commander," the king says.

The young man beside the throne steps forward. Close up, he's built like a door, broad and square, with black hair pulled back so tightly it looks painful. The scar along his jaw is old and pale. His eyes look much older than the rest of him.

"Cut the rope."

"It will be done."

His voice is completely flat. He draws the curved bronze sword at his hip, and before I can flinch, the blade flicks down between my wrists. The rope falls away in two pieces.

The edge passed so close that I felt the cold of it on my skin. It didn't touch me.

*That was a test,* I think, rubbing my raw wrists. *Or a warning. Or both.*

Commander Nakhtmin sheathes his sword and steps back to his place beside the throne, and doesn't look at me again.

***

The doors at the far end of the hall bang open.

A man stumbles in between the columns. He's covered in dust from his sandals to his hair, so grey with it he looks like a statue that's learned to run. Two guards are holding his arms, half helping, half dragging.

He drops to his knees on the painted floor and kisses it, and then he lifts his head and gasps out his message before the herald can even announce him.

"Majesty. From the garrison in the north. The men of Hatti have come down again. Another city that sent tribute to Kemet has opened its gates to them. Its prince has sworn to their king. The roads north are closed."

The hall erupts.

Now they do shout. Men in gold collars push forward between the columns, talking over each other. *Hatti again. That's the third city. Send the army. We have no army to send, the treasury is empty. Then empty the temples. The temples are being rebuilt, by the king's own decree—*

Amenhotep raises his staff. Not high. Just a little.

The shouting stops.

"The council will hear it," he says gently. "In private. The king is tired."

Nobody asks the king whether he's tired.

But I'm not listening to the Vizier anymore.

I'm standing at the bottom of the dais steps, small and filthy and forgotten, and in my head my mother's chart is unrolling itself across the wall above my bed. The neat columns of names. The dates. The notes in the margins in her tiny, careful hand.

*Amarna letters. Vassal kings in Canaan and Syria begging the pharaoh for help. Pharaoh doesn't answer. City after city goes over to the Hittites.*

*The Restoration Stela. Carved in this king's name. "The gods had turned their backs on this land. If an army was sent to Djahy to widen the borders of Egypt, it had no success."*

*No success.*

I know this.

I know what's coming. Not all of it. Not the details. But I know which way the wind blows in this reign, because I grew up reading the end of the story. I know which wars go badly. I know which names last and which names get cut off the walls with chisels.

I know when this boy dies.

Every grown man in this hall is arguing about what will happen next, and none of them knows.

I do.

*A child can't hold a sword,* I think. *A child can't command a soldier, or sit on a council, or ask who really gave the order without ending up in the shadows behind the columns.*

*But a child who knows what happens next…*

*That child would be worth keeping alive.*

My hands are trembling again. Not from fear this time.

***

"Come here."

The voice is very quiet, meant only for me.

The court is still muttering over the messenger. Nobody is watching the bottom of the dais. I climb two steps, then three, until I'm level with the painted footstool and the little gold sandals dangling above it.

The king leans down toward me. Up close, he smells of myrrh and of something else, plain and warm, like bread.

"What is your name?"

"Kael."

He tries it. "Kael." He says it carefully, like a word in a language he doesn't speak. It's the first time anyone in this world has said my name.

Then he says:

"You looked at him before you looked at me."

I go cold.

"Everyone looks at me first," the boy says. His voice is flat and soft and perfectly calm. "Then they look at him. To see if they were allowed to." He tilts his head. "You didn't look to see if you were allowed. You looked to *watch* him."

"……"

The softness I saw in his face a few minutes ago is gone. In its place is something I wasn't expecting at all. His dark eyes move over me slowly, from my mud-caked hair to my bare feet and back again, and they're not the eyes of a lonely child.

They're the eyes of a player looking at a new piece on the board. Weighing what it's worth. Deciding where to put it.

*He didn't just save me,* I realise. *He acquired me.*

*Because he saw what I saw. A boy in a room full of men who want to own him.*

*And he wants something of his own.*

"Don't die quickly, Kael," the king of Egypt says. "Pharaoh is tired of new playmates."

Then he sits back on his enormous throne, and his face goes blank and polite and nine years old again, and he looks out over his arguing court as if I'm not there at all.

We are more alike, he and I, than anyone in this hall suspects.

I'm not sure yet whether that's going to save me or get me killed.

***

The soldier from the wharf takes me away.

He doesn't drag me this time. He doesn't need to. He just walks, and I follow, because I'm the king's property now, and property follows.

As we pass between the columns, a courtier in a heavy wig leans to the man beside him and murmurs, not quietly enough, "The Pharaoh's Toy."

The other man laughs into his hand.

I keep walking. I count.

One hundred and twelve steps from the throne-room doors. Left at a painted pillar. Right past a courtyard with a dry fountain. Down three steps into a corridor that smells of lamp oil and old bread, where the walls are bare plaster and the doors are plain wood.

Servants' quarters. Close to the throne room. Close enough to be called when wanted.

The soldier stops at a heavy cedar door and pushes it open.

The room behind it is narrow, no wider than a man with his arms spread. A woven reed mat on the floor. A clay bowl of water. A single slit of a window, high up, letting in a bar of red evening light that lies across the floor like a blade.

He puts one hand between my shoulders and shoves, not hard. I stumble in and drop onto the mat.

He stands in the doorway for a moment, looking down at me. He's young, I realise. Maybe nineteen. He has a sunburned nose and tired eyes, and I don't think he's cruel. I think he's just someone who has stopped expecting things to end well.

"Sleep, little pet," he says. "Enjoy it while you can."

He starts to close the door. Then he stops, and adds, almost kindly:

"The last one lasted eleven days."

The door shuts.

A bar slides across it, on the outside.

***

I sit on the reed mat in the dark.

The bar of red light on the floor goes purple, then grey, then nothing. Somewhere far off in the palace, a gong sounds, deep and slow, and the sound rolls through the walls and into my chest and fades.

I'm still wearing the blue shirt with red sleeves. It's stiff with dried mud. I can smell the river on my skin, and myrrh in my hair, and under it all, very faint, smoke. Old smoke. From my mother's books.

I pull my knees up to my chest. They're very small knees.

Somewhere in this palace, a man with linen on his fingers has my ten-thousand-yen note in a cedar box. Somewhere, a priest is putting his scales away for the night. Somewhere, a servant girl is wringing out a cloth. Somewhere, a nine-year-old king is lying awake in a bed too big for him, thinking about the new piece he's just put on his board.

And somewhere, very far away, a god with a face made of dust is watching a little hourglass.

*Tick.*

I think of the boy on the cherry road, laughing on the wet pavement in his blue shirt.

I think of my own voice, under the oldest tree, saying it to no one.

*I wish I was ten again. Life was so much simpler.*

I wished for a simpler life…

…and the gods gave me a palace full of people who want me dead.
