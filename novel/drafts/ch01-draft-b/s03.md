**Thebes. Midday.**

The heat has its hand on the back of my neck before I've opened my eyes.

I'm lying on packed earth in a slot between two walls. Mud brick, the colour of a loaf, close enough on either side that I could touch both if my arms were longer. They don't seem to be. A strip of sky runs along the top of the slot, and it's a blue I've only seen on paint charts.

I sit up, and the ground is very close to my face.

That's not a figure of speech. I'm sitting, and the earth is right there, and my knees are right there, and they're the wrong knees. Round. Scabbed on the left. Bare, under a pair of shorts too faded to have been bought this decade.

Above the shorts, a blue shirt with red sleeves, and a label inside the collar scratching the exact place on my neck it used to scratch when I was ten.

I'd forgotten the label. The body hadn't.

My hands are a child's. Short fingers, no calluses, a bitten thumbnail I stopped biting at thirteen. The bracelet my mother wove for me, red once, now the colour of rust, is still on my left wrist, but it's slack. It turns around the bone when I move.

*You keep what you carried nearest your heart.*

There's paper in the breast pocket. I take it out and look at it for a while. A ten-thousand-yen note, folded in half and in half again. The old man printed on it looks back with the expression of someone who has also been having a strange morning.

"Okay," I say, to see.

What comes out is a little boy's voice. Clear and high, and so exactly like a recording of myself at ten that my skin crawls.

I put the bill back. I put my hand flat on the warm wall. Then I stand, which takes two tries, because my balance is sitting about forty centimetres lower than I keep it, and I walk toward the noise at the end of the slot.

***

The noise is a market, and the market is the biggest thing I've ever stood at the edge of.

Linen awnings on poles, hundreds of them, sagging in the heat, sloping down a long dusty square toward a river. The river is brown and slow and so wide the far bank is a line. Barges sit against a stone wharf, low in the water, and men with sacks on their shoulders go up the steps in a chain, singing something with a lot of the same word in it.

Past the river, a strip of green so bright it hurts. Past the green, desert. Past the desert, a wall of pale cliff.

I know the cliffs. I've seen them in a hundred photographs, the ones taken from the air, with the little road snaking in. I've never seen them from the wrong side.

Upriver, a stone wall as high as an apartment block runs along the bank, and behind it stands a needle. One shaft of stone with its point sheathed in something that throws the sun back like a signal. The textbooks had it grey and pitted. Nobody told me it was ever this.

My mother would have told me the name of the city, and she'd have used the old one, because she didn't believe in letting the Greeks have the last word.

*Waset.* The city of Amun. The god's house, behind that wall, with the sun on its tip.

So the river is the Nile, and this is Thebes, and the god in the dunes wasn't exaggerating when he said he'd choose.

Something lands on my cheek. A fly. I brush it off and another takes its place, and I think, with the part of my brain that was doing past papers eleven hours ago, that there is nothing in any book that prepares you for the flies.

***

I go in.

A market is mostly knees when you're this high. Linen kilts, bare brown legs, the swinging edge of a basket. A woman in a white sheath dress goes by with a jar on her head and doesn't look down. A man with two live geese by the necks nearly walks into me and swears at the geese.

Boys my size run between the stalls with their heads shaved except for one braid over the right ear. I've seen that on tomb walls a thousand times and never once on a living kid. Here it's on all of them, and I'm aware for the first time of the untidy hair on my own head, and don't yet know the half of it.

The shouting turns into words without my noticing when. A man with fish laid out on wet leaves swears they were swimming at dawn. Two women argue over a copper weight, and one of them uses a word I know from a glossary and have never heard said aloud. The beer stall smells like bread that's gone wrong.

Every word reaches me a half-beat late and lands heavy, like it was thrown.

*It'll feel like talking through a wall.*

The bread is what finds me.

The smell comes from under a stained awning, where a thick-armed woman is lifting flat loaves off the side of a clay oven with her bare fingers and stacking them. Rough brown bread with ash on the bottom. On the counter beside her, a wide blue-glazed jar, cracked down one side and brimming with water, and three cups.

I stop. The counter comes to my chest. My stomach makes a noise it hasn't made since I was small, because this body has never eaten, not once, and would like to start.

I lean on the counter and look into the jar to see how bad I look.

A child looks back.

Round face. Soft chin. Skin that's never seen a sun like this one. Hair falling over the forehead in rough yellow pieces, actually yellow, the colour of wheat in a museum painting.

The eyes are blue.

My eyes are brown. My eyes have always been brown.

There's one thing in the water I recognise. A white nick through the left eyebrow.

I don't jerk back. I hold on to the counter and look at the stranger with my scar until the water settles and the stranger looks steadily back.

*They see a child,* I think. *Fine. So do I.*

"You're putting your fingers in my water."

The woman. Flour to the elbows, a tattoo of a cat on the inside of one wrist.

"I'm sorry." My voice is a little bell. "One loaf, please."

Her face changes. The way you'd look at a goat if it said *please.*

"Says please. Where's your mother?"

"I'm on my own."

"What've you got, then?"

Barter. There are no coins here. Nobody's thought of them yet. Grain, linen, copper by the deben if you're rich, and I have none of those things, and I'm so hungry my hands are shaking.

There's a very small, very stupid voice in me that is ten years old. It says: *money is money. Show her.*

I take out the bill, unfold it, and hold it up.

***

She takes it between a floury finger and thumb and holds it to the light.

"What's this?"

"Money. Where I'm from."

"Where's that?"

I say nothing.

She turns it over. The old man in his high collar looks up at her. She squints at the numbers, and the lettering, and the little oval of colour in the corner, and her mouth goes tight.

"Whose face is this? Is this a king? He's got no crown." She holds it out sideways without looking. "Hor. What's this writing?"

Hor, with the fish, doesn't know. Neither does the woman with the figs, nor the two wet porters from the wharf, nor the old man who was buying bread when I came up and has been staring at my hair with his loaf forgotten in his hands.

They all come anyway. That's how a crowd works. One person points and twenty come to see what the pointing is for.

"Not papyrus."

"Look at the *colours* on it."

"Look at *him.* Look at his hair."

"*Khat-su,*" somebody says. It means sand-foreign. From the way he says it, it means *trash the wind blew in.*

"Spy," says somebody else, and that one travels.

*Spy for who? Hatti. They've got yellow-haired ones up there. They send children now. Who looks at a child? Get the temple men, the temple's right there—*

"I'm not a spy," I say, and nobody hears, because it's the voice of a ten-year-old and there are thirty people talking.

Over the heads I see two long staves coming, held upright, and under them two shaved heads and two spotless white kilts. Not soldiers. Not police. The granary with the ram-headed seals on its jars is thirty metres away, and these are the men who guard the god's grain, and they move through the crowd the way you move through tall grass.

The bread woman is already holding my bill out to them.

*That's mine,* I think, uselessly.

The first stave comes down, and I'm not under it.

***

Nine years of fencing, and all it's good for in this body is knowing which way to fall.

I go low, because I'm already low. Under the fig woman's basket, between the porters, past a donkey that doesn't care. A stave cracks on the counter behind me and the bread woman shouts about her jar. The temple men shout too, for people to make way, and people never make way fast enough. That's the one thing that's the same everywhere.

At the top of the wharf steps, a cart lies tipped on its side with its axle snapped, and a man squats beside it arguing with the wood. The broken end sticks out at the height of a grown man's shins.

I put both hands on it as I pass and shove.

It swings across the gap behind me. There's a sound like a sack of onions hitting the ground, and a great deal of swearing.

The second man comes round the cart from the other side.

There's nothing past me but the edge of the wharf and the drop.

I'd like to say I thought about it.

I go off the edge, and for a second there's nothing, and then the mud takes me to the knees. Cold. Black. It stinks of everything the river has ever been fed. I pitch forward onto my hands, and the stink is in my mouth, and when I lift my head there's a man standing in the shallows a few metres off with his kilt hitched round his thighs, rinsing mud off his shins, who has stopped rinsing to look at me.

Leather cap. A spear leaning on the wall beside him. A cowhide shield on the mud, black and white patches like a dairy cow. The look of a man who was enjoying a moment to himself.

My right hand goes to my hip, where a sword has never been, and finds shorts.

I try the thing anyway. The one thing I know. Weight back, feint left, go right.

My feet don't come out of the mud.

He doesn't even step. He puts one arm out and collects me by the front of my shirt the way you'd collect a bag someone threw at you, and holds me out at arm's length while I kick, and looks up at the top of the wall, where two white kilts have appeared.

"Yours?" he says.

"Temple's. He stole from the god's market."

"He paid with the Hittite's writing," the soldier says, as if correcting a child. "I heard it from here. Enemy writing goes to the palace. Go back to your grain."

The temple men look at each other.

Then they go back to their grain.

That's a whole lesson, and I've just had it hanging in the air by my shirt.

The spear butt in the stomach is almost an afterthought. He doesn't swing it. He brings it up to stop me kicking, and this body folds around it like a wet towel, and the air goes out of me so completely that for a moment I think it isn't coming back.

I'm on my face in the mud again.

"Stay," he says, like you'd say it to a dog, and goes to get the rope.

***

They tie my wrists in front of me with cord that bites, and walk me back up through the market, and I'll say this for being arrested in a crowd: it's a good way to see who's who.

People get out of the soldier's way without being asked. The city police at the corner, the Medjay, with their wrist bands and their patience, nod to him, and he doesn't nod back.

He steps aside, though, for a man in long white linen with a leopard skin over one shoulder, head and all, the small dead face lolling at his hip. The soldier drops his eyes. The priest doesn't trouble to notice.

At the granary gate a scribe sits cross-legged with a palette on his knees and makes a mark for every sack that goes in, and every sack is stamped with the same ram's head, and the man standing over the scribe isn't a soldier or a policeman. He's in white.

*Whoever owns the grain,* my father used to say, usually when somebody at a dinner party had made the mistake of asking him about Egypt, *owns Egypt. Everything else is theatre.*

The temple owns the grain. The soldier owns the temple's men, when it comes to it. Somebody owns the soldier.

I count, because I can't stop. Two hundred and forty paces from the wharf to the lane I woke up in. Three turns. One gate, two guards. One cracked blue jar.

The bread woman, when we pass her, doesn't look at me. She's counting the cups.

Nobody looks at me. They look at the rope. A boy on a rope is a story they already know.

*They see a child.* I keep saying it, like a hand on a rail. *They see a child, and a child is nothing, and nothing is the safest thing in this city to be.*

At the far end of the market there's a second wharf, and a low boat with a reed canopy, and a crew who've been waiting in the shade and aren't pleased to be fetched out of it. The soldier lifts me in by the back of the shirt and sets me down among the ropes.

We push off.

The water is wide and slow and full of light. The city slides back behind us with its wall and its gold needle. Ahead, past the green, there's a long low palace of white and painted columns, built right at the lip of the desert, on the side of the river where the Egyptians put their dead.

The sun is going down behind the cliffs. They turn the colour of a peach.

Somewhere behind those cliffs, in a dry valley with a road snaking into it, every king on my mother's chart is lying in the dark.

The one I'm being taken to isn't there yet.

I sit in the bottom of the boat with my tied hands between my knees and watch the cliffs change colour, and I don't think about anything at all, as hard as I can.
