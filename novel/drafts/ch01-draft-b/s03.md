**Thebes. Late afternoon.**

The heat has its hand on the back of my neck before I've opened my eyes.

I'm lying in a slot between two walls of mud brick, under a strip of sky so blue I've only seen it on paint charts. I sit up, and the ground is very close to my face.

That's not a figure of speech. My knees are right there, and they're the wrong knees. Round, scabbed on the left, bare under faded shorts. Above the shorts, a blue shirt with red sleeves, and a label inside the collar scratching the exact place on my neck it used to scratch when I was ten.

I'd forgotten the label. The body hadn't.

My hands are a child's. Short fingers, no calluses, a bitten thumbnail I stopped biting at thirteen. The bracelet my mother wove for me, red once, now the colour of rust, hangs slack on my wrist and turns around the bone when I move.

*You keep what you carried nearest your heart.*

There's paper in the breast pocket. A ten-thousand-yen note, folded in half and in half again. The old man printed on it looks back with the expression of someone who has also been having a strange morning.

"Okay," I say, to see.

A little boy's voice. Clear and high, and so exactly like a recording of myself at ten that my skin crawls.

I stand, which takes two tries, because my balance is sitting forty centimetres lower than I keep it, and walk toward the noise.

***

The noise is a market, and the market is the biggest thing I've ever stood at the edge of.

Linen awnings on poles, hundreds of them, sloping down a long dusty square to a river so wide the far bank is a line. Barges sit low against a stone wharf, and men with sacks on their shoulders go up the steps in a chain, singing something with a lot of the same word in it.

Past the river, a strip of green so bright it hurts. Past the green, desert. Past the desert, a wall of pale cliff, and above it the sun, halfway down from the top of the sky and sliding toward the rock. I've seen those cliffs in a hundred photographs taken from the air. I've never seen them from the wrong side.

Upriver, behind a wall as high as an apartment block, stands an obelisk with its tip sheathed in gold, throwing the sun back like a signal. The textbooks had it grey and pitted. Nobody told me it was ever this.

My mother would have used the old name for the city, because she didn't believe in letting the Greeks have the last word. *Waset.* The city of Amun. So the river is the Nile, and this is Thebes, and the god in the dunes wasn't exaggerating when he said he'd choose.

A fly lands on my cheek. I brush it off and another takes its place, and I think, with the part of my brain that was doing past papers eleven hours ago, that nothing in any book prepares you for the flies.

***

A market is mostly knees when you're this high. Linen kilts, bare brown legs, the swinging edge of a basket. The awnings have started throwing long shadows across the dust, and the heat hasn't noticed. A woman in a white sheath dress goes by with a jar on her head and doesn't look down.

Boys my size run between the stalls with their heads shaved except for one braid over the right ear, which I've seen on tomb walls a thousand times and never once on a living kid.

The shouting turns into words without my noticing when. A man with fish on wet leaves swears they were swimming at dawn. The beer stall smells like bread that's gone wrong. Every word reaches me a half-beat late and lands heavy, like it was thrown.

The bread is what finds me. Under a stained awning a thick-armed woman is lifting flat loaves off the side of a clay oven with her bare fingers, and beside her on the counter stands a wide blue-glazed jar, cracked down one side and brimming with water.

The counter comes to my chest. My stomach makes a noise it hasn't made since I was small, because this body has never eaten, not once, and would like to start.

I lean on the counter and look into the jar to see how bad I look.

A child looks back.

Round face. Soft chin. Skin that's never seen a sun like this one. Hair falling over the forehead in rough yellow pieces, actually yellow, the colour of wheat in a museum painting.

The eyes are blue. My eyes have always been brown.

There's one thing in the water I recognise. A white nick through the left eyebrow.

I hold on to the counter and look at the stranger with my scar until the water settles and the stranger looks steadily back.

*They see a child,* I think. *Fine. So do I.*

"You're putting your fingers in my water."

The woman. Flour to the elbows, a cat tattooed on the inside of one wrist.

"I'm sorry." My voice is a little bell. "One loaf, please."

Her face changes, the way you'd look at a goat if it said *please.* "Where's your mother?"

"I'm on my own."

"What've you got, then?"

Barter. There are no coins here; nobody's thought of them yet. I have no grain, no linen, no copper, and I'm so hungry my hands are shaking, and there's a very small, very stupid voice in me that is ten years old and says *money is money. Show her.*

I unfold the bill and hold it up.

***

She holds it to the light and turns it over. The old man in his high collar looks up at her, and her mouth goes tight.

"Whose face is this? He's got no crown. What's this writing?"

Nobody knows what the writing is, and they all come anyway, because that's how a crowd works: one person points and twenty come to see what the pointing is for.

"Not papyrus."

"Look at *him.* Look at his hair."

"*Khat-su,*" somebody says. It means sand-foreign. From the way he says it, it means *trash the wind blew in.*

"Spy," says somebody else, and that one travels. *Hatti. They've got yellow-haired ones up there. They send children now. Who looks at a child?*

"I'm not a spy," I say, and nobody hears, because it's the voice of a ten-year-old and there are thirty people talking.

Over the heads come two long staves, held upright, and under them two shaved heads and two spotless white kilts. Not soldiers. Not police. The granary with the ram-headed seals on its jars is thirty metres away, and these are the men who guard the god's grain.

The bread woman is already holding my bill out to them.

*That's mine,* I think, uselessly.

The first stave comes down, and I'm not under it.

***

Nine years of fencing, and all it's good for in this body is knowing which way to fall.

I go low, because I'm already low. Under a basket, between two porters, past a donkey that doesn't care. At the top of the wharf steps a cart lies on its side with its axle snapped, the broken end sticking out at shin height. I shove it as I pass, and it swings across the gap behind me, and there's a sound like a sack of onions hitting the ground and a great deal of swearing.

The second man comes round the cart from the other side, and a hand closes on the back of my collar. The label that's been scratching me since the lane tears away in his fist, and I'm through, and there's nothing past me but the edge of the wharf and the drop.

I go off the edge, and the mud takes me to the knees. Cold, black, stinking of everything the river has ever been fed. When I lift my head there's a soldier standing in the shallows a few metres off with his kilt hitched round his thighs, rinsing mud off his shins, who has stopped rinsing to look at me.

My right hand goes to my hip, where a sword has never been, and finds shorts.

I try the one thing I know anyway. Weight back, feint left, go right. My feet don't come out of the mud. He doesn't even step. He collects me by the front of my shirt the way you'd collect a bag someone threw at you, holds me out at arm's length while I kick, and looks up at the top of the wall, where two white kilts have appeared.

"Yours?"

"Temple's. He stole from the god's market."

"He paid with the Hittite's writing," the soldier says, as if correcting a child. "Enemy writing goes to the palace. Go back to your grain."

The temple men look at each other. Then they go back to their grain.

That's a whole lesson, and I've just had it hanging in the air by my shirt.

The spear butt in the stomach is almost an afterthought. He brings it up to stop me kicking, and this body folds around it like a wet towel, and the air goes out of me so completely I think it isn't coming back.

"Stay," he says, like you'd say it to a dog, and goes for the rope.

***

They tie my wrists and walk me back up through the market, and I'll say this for being arrested in a crowd: it's a good way to see who's who.

The city police at the corner, the Medjay, with their wrist bands and their patience, nod to the soldier, and he doesn't nod back. He steps aside, though, for a man in long white linen with a leopard skin over one shoulder, the small dead face lolling at his hip, and drops his eyes, and the priest doesn't trouble to notice. At the granary gate a scribe marks every sack that goes in, every sack carries the same ram's head, and the man standing over him is in white.

*Whoever owns the grain,* my father used to say, *owns Egypt. Everything else is theatre.*

Nobody looks at me. They look at the rope. A boy on a rope is a story they already know.

*They see a child.* I keep saying it, like a hand on a rail. *A child is nothing, and nothing is the safest thing in this city to be.*

At the far end of the market there's a boat with a reed canopy. The soldier lifts me in and sets me down among the ropes, and we push off, and the city slides back behind us with its wall and its gold needle.

He sits with his spear across his knees and says nothing. Once his eyes go to my wrist, to the frayed red cord turning on the bone, and stay there a moment. It's the kind of thing a mother ties on a child. He could take it; nobody would ask. He looks back at the water instead. Ahead, past the green, there's a long low palace of painted columns, built at the lip of the desert, on the side of the river where the Egyptians put their dead.

The sun has come down to a hand's width above the cliffs, and they've begun to turn the colour of a peach.

Somewhere behind them, in a dry valley with a road snaking into it, every king on my mother's chart is lying in the dark.

The one I'm being taken to isn't there yet.
