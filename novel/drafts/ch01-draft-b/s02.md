**Nowhere. No time.**

I come to on my back with grit between my teeth.

There's no sand on the cherry road. I know every square metre of that road. So before I open my eyes I already know I'm not on it, and I lie there a while with them shut, doing the thing where you check your body one piece at a time. Toes. Knees. The ribs, which should be flat. The spine, which should be a story doctors tell each other later.

Everything answers. Nothing hurts.

I'd have preferred pain. Pain means a hospital.

The sky, when I look at it, has no colour. Not dark, not light. It's the colour of the inside of your eyelids, and it goes on in every direction without a sun or a seam, and under it, to the horizon, there are dunes.

The sand is pale and very fine. I pick up a handful and let it run, and halfway through I stop, because it's not sand. I'm holding a hairpin. A baby tooth. A key, the small flat kind, for a lock that's long since been changed. Grains of things, worn down. The whole dune is like that, and the one past it, and the ones past those. Bigger things poke out here and there the way bones do in a real desert: a bicycle with the chain off. A dog's leash with no dog. In a dip just below me, a wedding ring catches the no-light, and while I'm looking at it, it goes. It doesn't sink. It fades, like a photograph left in a shop window, until there's only the dip.

Three metres off, half-buried, is a pair of reading glasses with the left arm mended with tape.

I know that tape. I did it. I was eleven and he was going to buy a new pair and I said I could fix them, and he wore them taped for four years out of a stubbornness I've since recognised in myself.

I don't pick them up. I'm not sure why. I think I'm afraid they'll fade while I'm holding them.

***

"You died, Kael."

I don't jump. I'd like to say that's composure. It's probably just that there's nothing left in me to jump with.

He's sitting on the crest of the next dune, where a second ago there was only dune, like a man on a wall waiting for a bus that stopped running years ago. I say *he*. I'm not sure. He's tall and narrow and wrapped in something pale that might be linen, and he's made of dust, the kind you see turning in a shaft of sun through a dirty window, gold and never still. There's a face. It keeps almost arriving. The dust gets as far as a brow, a cheekbone, the line of a mouth, and then thinks better of it.

His voice is dry and not loud. Pages turning in a library after it's closed.

"The tree was older than you," he says. "Roots like that go a long way. It fell. That's all it was."

"Who are you?"

"Whoever you like. People have been very creative about it." One hand, or the idea of one, moves to take in the dunes. "This is mine. All of it. Everything that's finished. Somebody has to keep it."

"The past."

"If you need a job title."

I look at the ring-shaped dip in the sand.

"That's what it looks like," he says, following my eye, "when the last person who remembers something stops. From this side. You'd be surprised how quiet it is."

***

I should be screaming. I'm aware of that. There's a part of me doing it, a long way down, with its hands over its face. But the part on top is the part that sat in a police station at four in the morning answering questions about accelerants while a woman in a cardigan kept offering me a juice box, and that part learned something that spring, which is that if you go cold enough, you can get through nearly anything, and worry about what it cost you later.

So I go cold.

"If I'm dead," I say, "why are we talking?"

"Because you asked me for something."

"I didn't ask you for anything."

"You stood on my step and said it out loud." He tilts his almost-head. "The little house under the tree. With the door. People used to leave things. Rice, mostly. Nobody's left anything in a long time, but the old woman sweeps, which I appreciate more than she knows."

The shrine. The circle with the line through it. A clock with its hands gone, my six-year-old self decided, and was wrong.

"I wasn't talking to you. I wasn't talking to anyone. People say that. It's a *thing people say.*"

"*I wish I was ten again. Life was so much simpler.*" He gives it back to me in my own voice, and it's worse than I remember. It sounds like whining. "You said it on my step, at an hour when I happened to be listening, which is rarer than you'd think. I've decided to say yes."

"You can't."

"Then you should be more careful where you stand."

He holds up his hand, and there's an hourglass in it that wasn't there before. A small one, the glass clouded, the frame some dark wood worn pale at the corners. The sand in it is the same gold as him.

"You'll be a child," he says. "Ten, since you were specific. Somewhere that's finished. I'll choose where. Two years." He turns the hourglass over, and a grain lets go with a sound I'd never have heard anywhere else. *Tick.* "Two floods of the river. When the second one goes down, I take my hand off you, and you go back exactly where you were."

Where I was. On my back on the wet pavement under eighty years of cherry tree, with my phone going.

"You're lending me two years and then sending me back to die."

"You're already dead. That part's done." He says it kindly, which I don't forgive him for. "I'm lending you some time I happen to have. It isn't really mine to lend. Try not to waste it."

"And if I don't want it."

He doesn't answer that. The sand keeps falling.

***

"Then tell me the rules."

My hands have started to shake, and I put them under my arms so he won't see, which is stupid, because I don't know what he sees.

"Where. When. How do I eat. What happens if I—"

"They'll understand you, and you'll understand them. It'll feel like talking through a wall. They'll hear the wall, too. That can't be helped." He counts it off on dust-fingers, the way a landlord goes through a lease. "You keep what you carried nearest your heart. Nothing else. Don't ask me to bend that; I don't know how."

"That's it?"

"That's a great deal. I've been generous. You'll see."

"Why me?"

He goes quiet.

I'd thought the quiet before was quiet. It wasn't. Somewhere behind me a dune sighs, a slow collapse, a thousand small forgotten things settling. He looks past me, down the slope, at the glasses with the taped arm.

Then he says, in my mother's voice, exactly, the pitch and the catch in it and all:

"*Don't let them forget his name.*"

I'm on my feet. I don't remember getting there.

"She said it to me first," he says, in his own voice again. "On the grass. She was more polite about it than you've been."

"Whose name? *Whose?* What did she—"

"Simple?" the god says, and the dust finally manages a mouth, and it's smiling. "We shall see."

The dune behind him comes apart. Not falling; lifting. A wall of gold grains rising into that colourless sky and bending toward me like a wave that's decided, and I get one breath, and it's full of other people's lost things.

Then it's on me, and I'm going down, and I'm going *in*. My sleeves swallow my hands. My collar slides past my chin. Something is folding me up like a letter, shorter and shorter, and the blazer is a tent and then it isn't anywhere, and the last sound before the sound of all that sand is small and patient and very close to my ear.

*Tick.*

Light.

White, flat, enormous light, and heat with it, and a smell so thick it has texture.

Fish. Smoke. River.
