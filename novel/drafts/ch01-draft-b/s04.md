**The palace. Sunset.**

The soldier doesn't bother with the ladder.

The cell is a square pit cut into rock under some back corner of the palace, with a wooden grate across the top. He holds me out over the hole the way you'd hold a bag over a bin, and lets go. It's about three metres. I land on my hip and my shoulder and the side of my head, in that order, and the grate bangs shut while I'm still finding out which part hurts most.

I lie where I landed. The floor is stone and cool, which is the first good thing that's happened to me today. Through the grate the light comes down in squares, orange going red, laid high on the far wall where the sun has pushed them. The day is nearly done. I am, I notice in a distant way, crying. This body does it without consulting me, hot and steady and without a sound.

*Soren,* I think. Sunday was going to be three years, and now I'm not going to go in a way I can't take back.

"That's a long way to fall, for someone so small."

There's a man sitting against the far wall with his knees drawn up. Old, seventy at least, thin, with the grey stubble of a shaved scalp that's been let go for a day or two. His kilt was good linen once. His hands are loose in his lap, untied.

"I didn't see you," I say. My voice comes out formal. It does that when I'm frightened.

His eyebrows go up. "You talk like a scribe's grandfather."

"I was raised by old people."

He laughs, dry and kind. "What did you do, grandfather?"

"I tried to buy bread. With the wrong thing."

He laughs again, and has to wipe his eyes, and I see that he's been crying too and is better at hiding it.

"And you?"

"I asked a question." He says it like a man confessing to a very small theft. "An order came down under the king's seal, taking the river gangs out of my district and giving them to the god's house for the building. In the month the dykes must be cut.

"So I went to the hall, and I knelt, and I asked the Lord of the Two Lands whether it was his own word."

"That's all?"

"You'd be surprised what *all* costs, in there."

*He didn't ask if the order was fair,* I think. *He asked who wrote it.*

"What happens now?"

"Now they weigh my heart." He says it the way you'd say it's going to rain. "And after, somebody goes to my tomb with a chisel and takes my name off the wall, so the gods can't find me when I call." A small old man's shrug. "That's the real death, grandfather. Not the blade. Being nobody's to remember."

*Don't let them forget his name.*

I don't say anything. There isn't anything.

He looks at me a while. Then he shifts his knees under him, slowly, an old man's joints, and shows me.

"Like this. Knees, then hands, then the face to the floor, and you stay there until you're spoken to. Don't look up early. Looking up early is what they remember."

I copy him. The stone is cold through the shorts.

"Good," he says. "Forty years I carried the fan, and that's the whole of what I learned. Keep it."

"I taught him to ride," the old man says after a while, not to me. "The boy. He was six. His hands were good on the reins. I told him so, and he was so pleased he told the whole court."

I sit down beside him, and neither of us says another word until a gong sounds above us, one slow note.

***

Two palace men bring us up together, through a kitchen court that smells of goose fat and smoke, where a boy my size turning a spit sees my hair and forgets to turn it, along a colonnade where servants flatten themselves against the wall and keep their eyes on the floor, through cedar doors so tall they have smaller doors cut into them for ordinary use.

Then the hall.

I've stood in the ruins and tried to picture the roof. This is what I was trying to picture. Two rows of columns as thick as the cherry tree march away into lamplight, each painted as a bundle of lotus stems with the flowers opening at the top, and over the flowers a gold ceiling, a long way up, with the smoke of a hundred lamps hanging under it in sheets.

Myrrh. I know the smell from little bags in the museum shop, and it's the same and it's nothing like it, the way a photograph of the sea is like the sea.

Men stand between the columns, dozens, in white linen and gold collars and heavy black wigs, and every one of them turns his head as we come in.

At the foot of the steps a scribe kneels with a palette on his knee and a reed in his hand, writing, and doesn't turn his head at all. Whatever happens in here goes down on papyrus.

The floor is painted. That's what gets me. Somebody plastered this whole floor and painted a marsh on it, blue water and bending reeds and ducks going up out of the papyrus with their wings wide, and I'm walking across it on a rope with river mud between my toes.

I'm already going down the way he showed me when a hand lands on the back of my neck and finishes it for me.

"Smell the earth before the Lord of the Two Lands."

My nose is on a painted duck.

***

A staff hits the stone three times. A herald reads out titles, a great many, and then the names that go in the ovals, and the throne name comes first, and it's one I can see in my mother's writing with my eyes shut.

*Nebkheperure.*

I stop breathing. My father's hand on my shoulder in front of the glass. *He was your age, Kael.* And the other date, at the bottom of the box.

They take the old man forward first. He goes on his knees at the foot of the dais, and I can see him along the floor, and his hands are shaking now. They weren't in the pit.

"The fan-bearer on the king's right," says a voice I haven't heard yet, "asked this hall whether the king's seal was the king's word."

The voice is soft. Soft and deep and perfectly polite, like a host at dinner, and it doesn't need to be loud, because the whole hall has already leaned toward it. I understand before I've seen the man that this is what everyone else in the room is arranged around.

"He asked the Lord of the Two Lands, to his face, whether the Lord of the Two Lands is lying." A pause with nothing hurried in it. "Order before mercy. Weigh him."

A priest comes out from between the columns, lean, shaved, a leopard skin over one shoulder, carrying a balance, two little pans on chains, and sets it on the painted water.

"Wennefer," the old man says beside me, with almost no voice. The High Priest of Amun. The god whose bread I tried to buy. A white feather in one pan. From the same box, a red stone roughly carved into the shape of a heart, in the other.

The Weighing of the Heart. In every tomb painting I've ever studied, it happens to the dead. Nobody in this room is dead yet.

The stone goes down. There's no trick that I can see, and that's the trick: nobody here is waiting to find out what the scale says, only for it to say it, so that it was the god and not them.

The priest's eyes go to the soft-voiced man before he speaks. Just for a second.

"Heavy. The heart is heavy with disorder."

"Ammit take it," the columns murmur. "Ammit take it."

The old man looks up the steps of the dais, past the soft voice, at whoever is sitting above it, and says, clearly, in the voice you'd use to remind a child of a promise:

"Nebkheperure. I held your reins."

Two guards take him under the arms. He doesn't fight them. He's seventy and he carried a feather behind kings for a living and he knows exactly where this ends, and he lets them walk him, and he looks back over his shoulder the whole way, until the shadow between the columns takes him.

The sound is wet, and heavy, and once.

A girl comes out of the same shadow with a bucket and a rag and starts on the floor. The rag going back and forth is the only sound in the hall, and nobody is in any hurry to cover it.

I've got both tied hands flat on the painted water. It's the only way to keep them still.

*An hour ago he was telling me about a boy with good hands on the reins.*

*I never asked his name.*

***

"The foreigner."

I'm hauled up onto my knees. The soldier from the river bows to the man at the throne's left hand. "Commander Nakhtmin. Taken at the god's market. He paid for bread with this."

He holds up my bill by one corner, stained brown with mud, and the market's words go round again. *Cipher. Hittite. Look at his hair. A child's the perfect spy.*

"Majesty." A heavy man with a heavier collar bows until his wig nearly brushes the painted reeds. "A foreigner, taken in the god's own market with the enemy's writing in his hand, and not even the wit to hide it. Let his heart be weighed."

"Let his heart be weighed," say the columns.

The priest hasn't even reset the balance. He steps back to it and puts his hand on the bar.

I count without wanting to. Eleven men between me and the doors, and I couldn't outrun one of them. The guards who took the old man are coming back out of the shadow, wiping their hands. One of them hasn't got it all.

The soft voice says nothing. I look for it now. He stands at the throne's right hand, the tallest man in the room and the thinnest, so thin the indigo robe hangs off him like it's on a hook, gold thread all over it spelling the god's name, Amun, over and over.

A shaved head, one band of gold across the brow. Three scars on each cheekbone, too even to be an accident. A tall staff with a falcon's head. His hands are wrapped in strips of linen, like a man with burns, and over the linen he wears more rings than fingers. One thumbnail, where the linen stops, is black to the root.

The eyes are hazel, river water with the sun in it, gone nearly gold in the lamps, and they move round the room resting nowhere, like a man reading a page he's read before.

"The Vizier," someone whispers near my shoulder. "Amenhotep."

I go through the chart, twice, fast. There's a God's Father called Ay, who I'd have expected to be standing exactly where this man is standing. There's a general. There are viziers somewhere in the small print, and I don't have their names.

*Either my parents were wrong, or this is a man history forgot to write down. And I just watched what happens to people who get written down.*

At the throne's other hand stands the Commander, broad, early twenties, black hair pulled back so hard it must hurt, with a curved bronze sword at his hip, a scar along the jaw, and eyes that look about forty. He hasn't moved since I came in.

*Think.* My heart is going so hard I can see it at the edges of my eyes. *What does a child have that a man doesn't? Not pity; you watched pity leave. Not mercy; mercy got its heart weighed.*

"I'm not a spy."

It rings. That's the trouble with this voice: it's a bell, and the hall was built to carry one. And it comes out wrong, the sentences too built, the words too old, out of a face with the softness still in it. A little boy who's swallowed a scribe.

"I have no people. I was raised by herders in the desert, far to the east. I came a long way, and I was robbed on the road, and that paper is all I have, and I would never—"

The hand on my neck hits me across the back of the head, hard enough that the hall goes white and comes back.

"You don't speak before the king unless the king bids you."

But it isn't the slap the room is reacting to. It's the voice. Two men step back from me. One makes a sign with his fingers against something. And the Vizier's hazel eyes have stopped going round the room and settled, for the first time, on me.

I lift my head anyway. There's nowhere else to put it.

The throne is gold, lion-armed, lion-footed, winged in blue and red stone. It's the one from the glass case. It was built for a god, and the boy sitting in it doesn't fill the seat.

He's nine. I've known he was nine since I was six. Slight and fine-boned and brown, with a narrow, serious face under the striped blue-and-gold nemes and the gold cobra rearing off his brow. Somebody has tied a braided false beard to his chin on a cord that's rubbed him red under the jaw. His feet don't reach the footstool. They hang above it in small gold sandals, and the footstool is painted with a row of foreigners on their faces, arms bound behind them, so that when the king's feet do reach, they'll rest on his enemies.

On his collar there's a scarab of black stone. His fingers are closed around it so hard the knuckles have gone pale.

He isn't looking at me. He's looking at the Vizier, with the look of every kid in every classroom I've sat in: watching the teacher's face to find out what the answer is going to be.

*He didn't give the order. The old man was right. And the boy knows it, and he sat there, with the man who taught him to ride being scrubbed off the floor in front of him.*

The priest's hand tightens on the bar.

The king of Egypt turns his head, as if someone has said his name, and looks down the steps, and finds a muddy foreign boy on his knees looking straight back up at him.

Neither of us looks away. His eyes are dark and ringed with black paint and much too tired for nine, and for a long moment he just looks, the way you'd catch your own reflection in a shop window and not be sure, for a second, that it's you.

Then his fingers come off the scarab.
