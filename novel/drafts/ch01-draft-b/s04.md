**The palace. Late afternoon.**

The soldier doesn't bother with the ladder.

The cell is a square pit cut down into rock under some back corner of the palace, with a wooden grate across the top and a ladder that lives on the outside. He gets me under the arms, and holds me out over the hole the way you'd hold a bag over a bin, and lets go.

It's about three metres. I land on my hip and my shoulder and the side of my head, in that order, and the grate bangs shut above me while I'm still finding out which part hurts most.

A bar slides. Sandals walk away.

I lie where I landed.

The floor is stone and cool, which is the first good thing that's happened to me today. There's a square of light from the grate, lying on the far wall, with the shadow of the bars across it. I watch it for a while. It's easier than thinking. There's a smell of old straw and older urine, and I am, I notice in a distant way, crying, which this body does without consulting me, hot and steady and without any sound.

*Soren,* I think. Just the name. Sunday was going to be three years, and I wasn't going to go, and now I'm not going to go in a way I can't take back.

Six books in brown paper on a shelf at the back of a tea shop.

A broom, stopped.

I lie there until the square of light has moved the width of my hand across the wall. Then I sit up, because my mother used to say the trouble with crying was that it felt like doing something, and get on with it.

Twenty-two blocks across. Nineteen up. Someone before me scratched a boat into the wall at head height with their fingernail, a decent boat, with a sail. Someone else scratched out the sail. The grate is pegged on the outside, nothing to do about that, and the walls were cut by people who didn't want them climbed. I check anyway. I'd have thought less of myself if I hadn't.

Then I sit with my back to the boat and wait, and a gong sounds somewhere above me, slow, one note, and the stone takes it up and holds it a long time after it should have stopped.

***

Two of them come for me. Not the soldier. Palace men, in better linen, with the bored, careful hands of people who move prisoners for a living.

Up the ladder, into a courtyard where the light is going gold and a girl is scattering water on the paving to keep the dust down. Through a kitchen court that smells of goose fat and smoke, where a boy my size turning a spit looks at my hair and forgets to turn it. Along a colonnade where servants flatten themselves against the painted wall to let us by and keep their eyes on the floor the whole way. Through a pair of cedar doors so high they have their own small doors cut into them for ordinary use.

Then the hall.

I've seen the ruins. Columns broken off at the knee, the paint gone, the floor dust. You stand in the Karnak hypostyle and tilt your head back and try to imagine the roof, and you can't, not really, nobody can. I'm telling you now that the imagining is nothing like it.

It's a forest made of stone. Two rows of columns as thick as the cherry tree march away from me into lamplight, each one painted as a bundle of lotus stems in blue and green and white with the flowers opening at the top, and over the flowers a ceiling, gold, a long way up, with the smoke of a hundred bronze lamps hanging under it in sheets. The smoke is myrrh. I know the smell from the museum shop, where they sold it in little bags, and it's the same and it's nothing like it, the way a photograph of the sea is like the sea.

Men stand between the columns. Dozens. White linen, wide gold collars, the heavy black wigs that were a fashion and a formality both, and they stand without talking, the way people wait outside an exam hall, and every one of them turns his head as we come in.

The floor is painted.

That's what gets me, if I'm honest. Not the gold. Somebody plastered this entire floor, every metre of it, and painted a marsh on it, blue water and bending reeds and fish turning in the water and ducks going up out of the papyrus with their wings wide, and it's beautiful, it's one of the most beautiful things I've ever seen, and I'm walking across it on a rope with river mud drying between my toes.

A hand lands on the back of my neck and puts me down on it.

"Smell the earth before the Lord of the Two Lands."

My nose is on a painted duck.

***

I keep my face down. But you can see a long way along a floor, between people's legs, and you can hear everything.

A staff hits the stone three times somewhere ahead.

A herald reads out titles. A lot of them. *Strong bull, perfect of birth. He of the Two Ladies. Golden Horus. Lord of Appearances.* I know the shape of them from the chart, and then he gets to the names that go in the ovals, and the throne name comes first, and it's one I can see in my mother's writing with my eyes shut.

*Nebkheperure.*

I stop breathing. I don't choose to.

My father's hand on my shoulder in front of the glass. *He was your age, Kael.* Nine. And the other date, the one at the bottom of the box, the one I can't see the number nineteen without.

They're not bringing me forward yet. Somebody else is already up there.

I turn my cheek on the duck and look along the floor and I can see him: an old man on his knees at the foot of the dais, bare-headed, his wig on the floor beside him where it fell or was knocked, and beside the wig a long fan on a gilded pole with one white ostrich plume, which would mean something to me if I'd listened harder at dinner. His hands aren't tied. He's not that kind of prisoner. He's the kind who came in on his own feet.

"—only whether it is your word, Majesty," he's saying. He has a dry, courteous, exhausted voice. "The order takes the men from the river gangs in my district and gives them to the god's house for the building, in the month the dykes must be cut, and I ask, as I have the right to ask, whether it is the king's own word. That's all. I ask nothing else."

Nobody answers him for a long moment.

Then somebody does, and the voice is so soft that the whole hall leans in to hear it, and I understand before I've seen the man that this is the thing in the room everyone else is arranged around.

"The king's word is under the king's seal," the voice says. "And the seal is on the order. You have seen it. You have asked the Lord of the Two Lands, to his face, in his own hall, whether the Lord of the Two Lands is lying." A pause, with nothing hurried in it. "Order before mercy. Weigh him."

***

A priest comes out from between the columns with a thing I've seen in paint a hundred times and never in bronze.

A balance. Two little pans on chains from a bar, on a stand about as high as my chest. He sets it on the painted water in front of the old man, and from a box he takes a single white feather and lays it in one pan, and from the same box he takes a stone, red, carved roughly into the shape of a heart, and lays it in the other.

It's the Weighing. The heart against the feather of truth. I could draw the tomb painting from memory: the jackal-headed god steadying the scale, the ibis-headed one writing it down, the monster with the crocodile's face waiting by the pans with her mouth open for the hearts that come up heavy.

It's a thing that happens to the dead. In the underworld. That's the entire point of it.

Nobody in this room is dead yet.

The stone goes down. Of course it does. It's a stone, and the other pan has a feather in it. There's no trick to it that I can see, and that's the trick. Nobody in this hall is waiting to find out what the scale says. They're waiting for the scale to say it, so that it was the god and not them.

"Heavy," the priest says. "The heart is heavy with disorder."

A murmur between the columns, low, the sound a congregation makes.

"Ammit take it."

"Ammit take it."

The old man doesn't get up and he doesn't shout. He looks up the steps of the dais, past the soft voice, at whoever is sitting above it, and he says, quite clearly, in the voice you'd use to remind a child of something he'd promised:

"Nebkheperure. I held your reins."

Two guards take him under the arms.

He doesn't fight them. That's the part I'll have trouble with later. He's seventy and he counts dykes for a living and he knows exactly where this ends, and he lets them walk him, and he looks back over his shoulder the whole way, until the shadow between the columns takes him.

The sound is wet and heavy and once.

A girl comes out from the same shadow with a bucket and a rag and gets down on her knees at the edge of the lamplight and starts on the floor. The rag going back and forth is the only sound in the hall, and nobody is in any hurry to cover it.

I've got both my tied hands flat on the painted water. It's the only way to keep them still.

*He asked who wrote the order,* I think. *Not whether it was fair. Who wrote it. He thought it wasn't the king, and he said so to the king, and that's what they killed him for.*

*So who wrote it?*

***

"The foreigner."

I'm hauled up onto my knees.

The soldier from the river steps forward, bows to someone beside the throne, and says, in the voice of a man reading a label: "Taken at the god's market by the river. He paid for bread with this."

The bread woman's hands have been and gone. He has my bill. He holds it up by one corner, crumpled, stained brown with mud, and the whole room leans in the way the market leaned in, and I hear the same words going round, the same ones, as if the market sent them ahead on the boat.

*What is it. Whose face. Not papyrus. That's writing, that's cipher. Hittite. Look at his hair. A child. A child's the perfect spy, who looks at a child.*

"Majesty." A heavy man with a heavier collar steps out and bows until his wig nearly brushes the painted reeds. "The law is plain. A foreigner taken inside the city with the enemy's writing in his hand. Let his heart be weighed."

"Let his heart be weighed," say the columns.

The priest hasn't even moved the balance. The stone and the feather are still sitting where they were. He just steps back to it and puts his hand on the bar.

The soft voice says nothing. I look for it now.

He's standing at the foot of the dais, at the throne's right hand, and he's the tallest man in the room and the thinnest, so thin the indigo robe hangs off his shoulders like it's on a hook. Gold thread all over it in small figures that I know, with a lurch, are the god's name, Amun, over and over. His head is shaved and gleams, with one band of gold across the brow. There are scars on his cheekbones, three on each side, too even to be anything but on purpose. A collar of blue lapis and red carnelian. A tall staff with a falcon's head. His hands are wrapped in strips of linen, like a man with burns, and over the linen he wears rings, more rings than fingers.

The eyes are light. Hazel, nearly gold in the lamps. They're going round the room, resting nowhere, the way a man reads a page he's read before.

"The Vizier," someone whispers, near my shoulder. "Amenhotep."

I go through the chart. I go through it twice, fast, every name in my mother's handwriting. There's a God's Father called Ay, who I'd expected to be standing exactly where this man is standing. There's a general. There are viziers, somewhere in the small print, and I don't have their names, and the one I'm hearing isn't ringing anything.

*So either my parents were wrong,* I think, *or I'm in a room with a man history forgot to write down. And I just watched what happens to people who get written down.*

Beside the Vizier, at the throne's other hand, stands a wall. A broad young man, early twenties, with his black hair pulled back so hard it must hurt and a curved bronze sword at his hip, and a scar along the jaw, and eyes that look about forty. He hasn't moved since I came in. I'm not certain he's blinked.

The priest's hand is on the bar.

*Think.* My heart's going so hard I can see it at the edges of my eyes. *What does a child have that a man doesn't. Not pity; there's no pity in this room, you watched it leave. Not mercy. Mercy got its heart weighed. What have you got.*

"I'm not a spy."

It rings. That's the trouble with this voice. It's a bell, and the hall was built to carry a bell.

And it comes out wrong. I can hear it coming out wrong, the way the court hears it: the sentences too built, the words too old, the pauses in the places a man puts them, out of a face with the softness still in it. A little boy who's swallowed a scribe.

"I came from very far away. I was robbed on the road and left in your city with nothing, and that paper is the only thing I have, and I'd never in my life—"

The hand on my neck hits me across the back of the head, hard enough that the hall goes white and then comes back.

"You don't speak before the king unless the king tells you to speak."

But it's not the slap the room's reacting to. It's the voice. Two men step back from me. One makes a sign with his fingers against something. The Vizier's light eyes have stopped going round and settled, for the first time, on me.

I lift my head anyway. There's nowhere else to put it.

Up the steps of the dais. Past the indigo robe and the rings. Past the young man with the sword.

The throne's gold. All of it: gold leaf over carved wood, lion heads at the ends of the arms, lion feet, and across the back a pair of wings worked in blue stone and red stone and a sun disc between them. It's the one from the glass case. I'd know it anywhere. It was built for a god, or a man wanting to be sat in like one, and it's enormous.

The boy sitting in it doesn't fill the seat.

He's nine. I know he's nine. I've known since I was six. He's slight and fine-boned and brown, with a narrow, serious face, and the striped blue-and-gold cloth of the nemes falls to his shoulders with the gold cobra rearing off his brow, and someone has tied a braided false beard to his chin on a cord that's rubbed him red under the jaw. A collar of gold and lapis covers his thin chest like a breastplate. His feet don't reach the footstool. They hang above it in little gold sandals, and the footstool is painted, like everything here is painted, and what's painted on it is a row of foreigners on their faces with their arms bound behind them, so that when the king's feet do reach, they'll rest on his enemies.

On his collar, pinned, there's a scarab of black stone. His fingers are closed around it so hard the knuckles have gone pale.

He isn't looking at me, or at the priest, or at the stained bill. He's looking at the Vizier, and he has the look I've seen on every kid in every classroom I've ever been in, the one where you watch the teacher's face to find out what the answer's going to be.

*He didn't give the order,* I think. *The old man was right. And he knows the old man was right, and he just sat there.*

*And he's nine. And he's sitting on a chair built for someone twice his size with a dead man's name on the floor in front of him.*

The priest's hand tightens on the bar.

The king of Egypt turns his head, as if someone's said his name, and looks down the steps, and finds a muddy foreign boy on his knees looking straight back up at him.

Neither of us looks away.

His eyes are dark and ringed with black paint and much too tired for nine. For a long moment he just looks, the way you'd look at your own reflection in a shop window and not be sure for a second that it's you.

Then his fingers come off the scarab.
