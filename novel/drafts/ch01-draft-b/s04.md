**The palace. Late afternoon.**

The soldier doesn't bother with the ladder.

The cell is a square pit cut into rock under some back corner of the palace, with a wooden grate across the top and a ladder that lives on the outside. He gets me under the arms, holds me out over the hole the way you'd hold a bag over a bin, and lets go.

It's about three metres. I land on my hip and my shoulder and the side of my head, in that order, and the grate bangs shut above me while I'm still finding out which part hurts most.

A bar slides. Sandals walk away.

I lie where I landed. The floor is stone and cool, which is the first good thing that's happened to me today. There's a square of light from the grate lying on the far wall, with the shadow of the bars across it.

I am, I notice in a distant way, crying. This body does it without consulting me. Hot and steady and without any sound.

*Soren,* I think. Just the name. Sunday was going to be three years, and I wasn't going to go, and now I'm not going to go in a way I can't take back.

Six books in brown paper on a shelf at the back of a tea shop.

A broom, stopped.

"That's a long way to fall, for someone so small."

I go still.

There's a man sitting against the far wall with his knees drawn up. I hadn't seen him. He's old, seventy at least, thin, bare-headed, with the grey stubble of a shaved scalp that's been let go for a day or two. His kilt was good linen once. His hands are loose in his lap, untied.

"I didn't see you," I say.

My voice comes out formal. It does that when I'm frightened. Every word set down carefully, like stepping stones.

His eyebrows go up. "You talk like a scribe's grandfather."

"I was raised by old people."

He laughs. It's dry and kind and doesn't last. "What did you do, grandfather?"

"I tried to buy bread."

"With what?"

"The wrong thing."

He laughs again, longer this time, and then has to wipe his eyes with the heel of his hand, and I realise he's been crying too, and is better at hiding it.

"And you?" I ask.

He's quiet a while.

"I asked a question," he says. "An order came down under the king's seal, taking the river gangs out of my district and giving them to the god's house for the building. In the month the dykes must be cut. So I went to the hall, and I knelt, and I asked the Lord of the Two Lands whether it was his own word."

"That's all?"

"That's all." He looks at his hands. "You'd be surprised what *all* costs, in there."

*He didn't ask if the order was fair,* I think. *He asked who wrote it. He thinks it wasn't the king.*

My father used to say the only dangerous question in any court was *who actually decided this?* He said it at dinner, cheerfully, with his mouth full. I don't think he ever expected anyone to find out.

"What happens now?" I ask.

"Now they weigh my heart."

He says it the way you'd say it's going to rain.

"They'll put a feather on one side and a stone on the other, and the stone will go down, because it's a stone, and the priest will say I'm heavy with disorder, and then someone will do the rest in the shadow where the king can't quite see." He shrugs, a small old man's shrug. "And after, somebody will go to my tomb with a chisel and take my name off the wall, so the gods can't find me when I call."

"They do that?"

"That's the real death, grandfather. Not the blade. Being nobody's to remember."

*Don't let them forget his name.*

I don't say anything. There isn't anything.

"I taught him to ride," the old man says, after a while, not to me. "The boy. He was six. His hands were good. Light on the reins. I told him so, and he was so pleased he told the whole court."

He goes quiet again.

I sit down beside him with my back to the wall, and neither of us says another word until the gong sounds above us, one slow note, and the stone takes it up and holds it long after it should have stopped.

***

Two men come for us. Palace men, in better linen, with the bored, careful hands of people who move prisoners for a living.

They bring the old man up the ladder first. They lift me after him, and we go together.

Across a courtyard where the light is turning gold and a girl is scattering water on the paving against the dust. Through a kitchen court that smells of goose fat and smoke, where a boy my size turning a spit sees my hair and forgets to turn it. Along a colonnade where servants flatten themselves against the painted wall to let us pass and keep their eyes on the floor the whole way.

Through a pair of cedar doors so tall they have smaller doors cut into them for ordinary use.

Then the hall.

I've seen the ruins. Columns broken off at the knee, the paint gone, the floor dust. You stand there and tilt your head back and try to picture the roof, and you can't, not really. Nobody can.

This is what you were trying to picture.

A forest made of stone. Two rows of columns as thick as the cherry tree march away from me into lamplight, each one painted as a bundle of lotus stems in blue and green and white, the flowers opening at the top. Over the flowers, a ceiling, gold, a long way up, with the smoke of a hundred bronze lamps hanging under it in sheets.

The smoke is myrrh. I know the smell from the museum shop, where they sold it in little bags. It's the same and it's nothing like it, the way a photograph of the sea is like the sea.

Men stand between the columns. Dozens. White linen, wide gold collars, heavy black wigs. They stand without talking, the way people wait outside an exam hall, and every one of them turns his head as we come in.

The floor is painted.

That's what gets me, if I'm honest. Not the gold. Somebody plastered this entire floor and painted a marsh on it. Blue water and bending reeds, fish turning in the water, ducks going up out of the papyrus with their wings wide. It's one of the most beautiful things I've ever seen, and I'm walking across it on a rope with river mud drying between my toes.

A hand lands on the back of my neck and puts me down on it.

"Smell the earth before the Lord of the Two Lands."

My nose is on a painted duck.

***

I keep my face down. But you can see a long way along a floor, between people's legs, and you can hear everything.

A staff hits the stone three times somewhere ahead.

A herald reads out titles. A great many. *Strong bull, perfect of birth. He of the Two Ladies. Golden Horus. Lord of Appearances.* I know the shape of them from the chart. Then he gets to the names that go in the ovals, and the throne name comes first, and it's one I can see in my mother's writing with my eyes shut.

*Nebkheperure.*

I stop breathing. I don't choose to.

My father's hand on my shoulder in front of the glass. *He was your age, Kael.* Nine. And the other date, at the bottom of the box. The one I can't see the number nineteen without.

They take the old man forward first.

He goes on his knees at the foot of the dais. I can see him along the floor. Somebody has set his wig down beside him, and beside the wig, a long fan on a gilded pole with one white ostrich plume, which must have been his, and which somebody has decided he can look at but not hold.

His hands are shaking. They weren't, in the pit.

"The fan-bearer on the king's right," says a voice I haven't heard yet, "came before this hall and asked whether the king's seal was the king's word."

The voice is soft. That's the first thing about it. Soft and deep and perfectly polite, like a host at a dinner, and it doesn't need to be loud, because the whole hall has already leaned toward it. I understand before I've seen the man that this is what everyone else in the room is arranged around.

"He asked the Lord of the Two Lands, to his face, in his own hall, whether the Lord of the Two Lands is lying."

A pause with nothing hurried in it.

"Order before mercy. Weigh him."

***

A priest comes out from between the columns with a thing I've seen in paint a hundred times and never in bronze.

A balance. Two little pans on chains from a bar, on a stand about as high as my chest. He sets it on the painted water in front of the old man, and from a box he takes a single white feather and lays it in one pan, and from the same box a stone, red, roughly carved into the shape of a heart, and lays it in the other.

The Weighing. The heart against the feather of truth. I could draw the tomb painting from memory. The jackal-headed god steadying the scale. The ibis-headed one writing down the result. The monster with the crocodile's face waiting by the pans with her mouth open for the hearts that come up heavy.

It's a thing that happens to the dead. In the underworld. That's the whole point of it.

Nobody in this room is dead yet.

The stone goes down. Of course it does. It's a stone, and the other pan has a feather in it. There's no trick to it that I can see, and that's the trick. Nobody in this hall is waiting to find out what the scale says. They're waiting for the scale to say it, so that it was the god and not them.

The priest's eyes go to the soft-voiced man before he speaks. Just for a second. Then:

"Heavy. The heart is heavy with disorder."

A murmur between the columns, low, the sound a congregation makes.

"Ammit take it."

"Ammit take it."

The old man doesn't get up, and he doesn't shout. He looks up the steps of the dais, past the soft voice, at whoever is sitting above it, and he says, clearly, in the voice you'd use to remind a child of a promise:

"Nebkheperure. I held your reins."

Two guards take him under the arms.

He doesn't fight them. That's the part I'll have trouble with later. He's seventy and he counts dykes for a living and he knows exactly where this ends, and he lets them walk him, and he looks back over his shoulder the whole way, until the shadow between the columns takes him.

The sound is wet, and heavy, and once.

A girl comes out of the same shadow with a bucket and a rag and gets down on her knees at the edge of the lamplight and starts on the floor. The rag going back and forth is the only sound in the hall, and nobody is in any hurry to cover it.

I've got both my tied hands flat on the painted water. It's the only way to keep them still.

*An hour ago he was telling me about a boy with good hands on the reins.*

*He asked who wrote the order. That's what they killed him for. So who wrote it?*

***

"The foreigner."

I'm hauled up onto my knees.

The soldier from the river steps forward and bows to the man standing at the throne's left hand. "Commander Nakhtmin. Taken at the god's market by the river. He paid for bread with this."

He has my bill. He holds it up by one corner, crumpled, stained brown with mud, and the hall leans in the way the market leaned in, and I hear the same words going round, as if the market had sent them ahead on the boat.

*What is it. Whose face. Not papyrus. That's cipher. Hittite. Look at his hair. A child. A child's the perfect spy. Who looks at a child.*

"Majesty." A heavy man with a heavier collar steps out and bows until his wig nearly brushes the painted reeds. "The law is plain. A foreigner taken inside the city with the enemy's writing in his hand. Let his heart be weighed."

"Let his heart be weighed," say the columns.

The priest hasn't moved the balance. The stone and the feather are still sitting where they were. He steps back to it and puts his hand on the bar.

*He didn't even reset it,* I think. *He isn't going to.*

I count without wanting to. Eleven men between me and the doors, and I couldn't outrun one of them. Two more at the doors. The guards who took the old man are coming back out of the shadow, wiping their hands.

The soft voice says nothing. I look for it now.

He's standing at the foot of the dais, at the throne's right hand. The tallest man in the room and the thinnest, so thin the indigo robe hangs off his shoulders like it's on a hook. Gold thread all over it in small figures that I know, with a lurch, are the god's name, Amun, over and over.

His head is shaved and gleams, with one band of gold across the brow. Three scars on each cheekbone, too even to be anything but on purpose. A collar of blue lapis and red carnelian. A tall staff with a falcon's head.

His hands are wrapped in strips of linen, like a man with burns, and over the linen he wears rings. More rings than fingers.

The eyes are light. Hazel, nearly gold in the lamps. They move round the room, resting nowhere, the way a man reads a page he's read before.

"The Vizier," someone whispers near my shoulder. "Amenhotep."

I go through the chart. Twice, fast, every name in my mother's handwriting. There's a God's Father called Ay, who I'd have expected to be standing exactly where this man is standing. There's a general. There are viziers somewhere in the small print, and I don't have their names, and this one isn't ringing anything.

*So either my parents were wrong, or I'm in a room with a man history forgot to write down.*

*And I just watched what happens to people who get written down.*

Beside the Vizier, at the throne's other hand, stands the Commander. A broad young man, early twenties, black hair pulled back so hard it must hurt, a curved bronze sword at his hip, a scar along the jaw. Eyes that look about forty. He hasn't moved since I came in. I'm not sure he's blinked.

The priest's hand is on the bar.

*Think.* My heart is going so hard I can see it at the edges of my eyes. *What does a child have that a man doesn't? Not pity. You watched pity leave. Not mercy. Mercy got its heart weighed. What have you got?*

"I'm not a spy."

It rings. That's the trouble with this voice. It's a bell, and the hall was built to carry a bell.

And it comes out wrong. I can hear it coming out wrong, the way the court hears it. The sentences too built. The words too old. The pauses in the places a man puts them, out of a face with the softness still in it. A little boy who's swallowed a scribe.

"I came from very far away. I was robbed on the road and left in your city with nothing. That paper is the only thing I have, and I would never in my life—"

The hand on my neck hits me across the back of the head, hard enough that the hall goes white and comes back.

"You don't speak before the king unless the king bids you."

But it isn't the slap the room is reacting to. It's the voice. Two men step back from me. One makes a sign with his fingers against something. And the Vizier's light eyes have stopped going round the room, and settled, for the first time, on me.

I lift my head anyway. There's nowhere else to put it.

Up the steps of the dais. Past the indigo robe and the rings. Past the young man with the sword.

The throne is gold. All of it. Gold leaf over carved wood, lion heads at the ends of the arms, lion feet, and across the back a pair of wings worked in blue stone and red stone with a sun disc between them. It's the one from the glass case. I'd know it anywhere. It was built for a god, or a man wanting to be sat in like one, and it's enormous.

The boy sitting in it doesn't fill the seat.

He's nine. I've known he was nine since I was six. He's slight and fine-boned and brown, with a narrow, serious face. The striped blue-and-gold cloth of the nemes falls to his shoulders, the gold cobra rearing off his brow, and someone has tied a braided false beard to his chin on a cord that's rubbed him red under the jaw. A collar of gold and lapis covers his thin chest like a breastplate.

His feet don't reach the footstool. They hang above it in small gold sandals. The footstool is painted, like everything here is painted, and what's painted on it is a row of foreigners on their faces with their arms bound behind them, so that when the king's feet do reach, they'll rest on his enemies.

On his collar there's a scarab of black stone, pinned. His fingers are closed around it so hard the knuckles have gone pale.

He isn't looking at me, or at the priest, or at the stained bill. He's looking at the Vizier. He has the look I've seen on every kid in every classroom I've ever sat in: watching the teacher's face to find out what the answer is going to be.

*He didn't give the order,* I think. *The old man was right. And the boy knows he was right, and he sat there.*

*He's nine. He's sitting on a chair built for someone twice his size, with the man who taught him to ride being scrubbed off the floor in front of him.*

The priest's hand tightens on the bar.

The king of Egypt turns his head, as if someone has said his name, and looks down the steps, and finds a muddy foreign boy on his knees looking straight back up at him.

Neither of us looks away.

His eyes are dark and ringed with black paint and much too tired for nine. For a long moment he just looks, the way you'd catch your own reflection in a shop window and not be sure, for a second, that it's you.

Then his fingers come off the scarab.
