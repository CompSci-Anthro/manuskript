The king raises one hand. Barely. Palm out, off the lion's head on the arm of the throne, a schoolboy asking leave in a room where asking is frowned on.

The priest stops with his hand on the scale. Sixty grown men in gold collars hold the same half-breath, and every one of them lets his eyes slide off the raised hand and down the steps to the foot of the dais, to find out what it means.

The Vizier doesn't look up. His face is pleasant and interested.

"Wait," the king says.

The voice is high and thin and shouldn't carry. It carries. It wobbles.

"Majesty." The heavy man bows again. "The weighing is the law. A foreigner with the enemy's—"

"I heard."

Two words. Flat. The way a child says *I heard* when he's been told to put his shoes on for the third time.

"Bring him up."

***

The hand on my neck walks me forward until I'm standing at the bottom of the steps with the painted footstool at the level of my eyes.

Up close he's smaller still. His wrists are sticks inside the gold. Under the kohl there are shadows, the kind you get from lying awake. He smells of myrrh and, under it, of something ordinary and warm, bread or milk, a little boy's smell.

He looks down at me from the throne of Egypt, and I look back, and I don't lower my eyes. I should. Everything I've learned in three years of offices and interviews says drop them, be small, be sorry. But I'm already small. I've never in my life been smaller. And I've just about run out of sorry.

So I look at him, and he looks at me. The muddy yellow hair. The strange shirt. The rope. The cut in my eyebrow.

Then he says, clearly, pitched to reach the back of the hall:

"I shall sentence you."

The priest's hand comes off the scale.

"By my royal authority, I declare you my royal playmate and pet."

***

For a second nobody gets it, me included.

Then the hall gets it, and the sound it makes isn't loud, courtiers don't shout, but it goes down the columns like something spilled. Shock. Disgust. Two short laughs, strangled off behind hands.

The heavy man has gone red to the wig. "Majesty, with respect. The boy is a foreigner. Possibly a sorcerer. He cannot simply be *kept*, like a monkey out of Punt—"

"He is mine now."

"Majesty, the weighing—"

"Pharaoh has sentenced."

The voice breaks clean in half on *Pharaoh*. His hand has gone back to the black scarab and shut on it, and I can see from here that it's shaking. The other hand has found the edge of the gold collar and is working at it.

He's terrified. He's nine years old and he's terrified and he's doing it anyway, and I feel something I haven't felt since I was fifteen and watching my own house burn, which is that I would like, very much, to put myself between somebody and a thing.

*You can't execute the king's property.*

The silence after *Pharaoh has sentenced* goes on long enough to hurt. Every face in the hall turns, not fast, to the foot of the dais.

The Vizier lets the boy's hand shake on the scarab one more time. Then he bows, and it's a lovely thing, the staff dipping, the rings going down in a wave.

"Userhat." Only the name, and the heavy man's mouth closes. "The Lord of the Two Lands has spoken," he says softly, to the room, "and his word is the seal. A toy for the king. Order before mercy, but a toy costs the Two Lands nothing."

He hasn't said a word to me, and he doesn't now. But as he comes up out of the bow his eyes come to me and stay, and narrow a little. He has found the small print.

I've been looked at by a lot of adults. Teachers. Detectives. Soren's uncle in Osaka, measuring how much of his spare room I'd take up. I know what it looks like when someone sees a kid.

This isn't it.

The soldier from the river passes my bill to the scribe at the foot of the steps, who holds it up, and the Vizier takes it between two linen-wrapped fingers and looks at the old man's face on it for exactly as long as he looked at mine.

"Enter it, and keep it with the records of this hall. Things from nowhere should be kept where they can be found again."

The scribe lays my ten thousand yen in a cedar box and shuts the lid. The last piece of the world I came from, and it was the Vizier who decided where it went.

"Nakhtmin."

The Commander comes down the steps and stands over me, and I find out he's even bigger from below.

"Cut the rope."

"It will be done."

There's nothing in the voice. The curved sword comes out and goes down between my wrists before I've seen him draw, and the cord falls in two pieces, and I feel the bronze go by. It didn't touch skin. He was never going to let it.

I rub my wrists. He wanted to know if I'd flinch.

***

The far doors bang.

A man comes in between the columns at a run that's become a stagger, grey with dust from hair to feet. He goes down on his face at the edge of the lamplight and gets his message out before the herald has drawn breath.

"Majesty. From the north. The men of Hatti have come down again. Another city that sent tribute to Kemet has opened its gates to them, and its prince has sworn to their king. The road past it is shut."

The hall comes apart. *Hatti again. That's the third this year. Send the army. There's no army to send; the god's house has had it all for the building. You'd take from Amun?*

The Vizier lifts his staff a hand's width, and it stops.

"The council will hear the messenger. Privately. The king is tired."

I've stopped listening. My mother's chart is unrolling itself down the wall over my bed, with all her small notes in the margins.

*Suppiluliuma. The Hittite. Takes everything north of the river that the last two kings let slip. The stela this boy puts up in the temple, with his own name on it: "If an army was sent to Djahy to widen the borders of Egypt, it had no success."*

I know this. Not the day, or the city, or the name of the prince who just changed sides. But I know which way this reign leans, because I was raised by two people who had read the end of it, and I know the number at the bottom of the box.

Sixty men in this room are shouting about what happens next, and not one of them knows.

I do.

*A child can't hold a sword,* I think, and my hands have started shaking again, and it isn't fear this time. *A child can't sit on the council, or ask who wrote the order. But a child who knows what's coming—*

*—would be worth keeping. For a while.*

I look up from the thought, and the Vizier is looking at me. Past the messenger, past the shouting, at the one face in the hall that didn't change when the news came in.

I put my eyes on the floor. Late, I think. Probably late.

***

"Come here."

It's very quiet. It's for me. The court is still loud over the messenger, the Vizier has turned his back to the dais to hear it, and nobody is watching the steps. I go up two, three, until I'm beside the painted footstool, and the king of Egypt leans down off his father's throne until his face is near mine.

"What are you called?"

"Kael."

He tries it. "Ka… el." He splits it in two, the way his language wants to, and the first half comes out as the word for a soul. Then he puts it back together, carefully, like a word in somebody else's language, and the sound of it goes through me, because it's the first time anyone in this whole world has said my name.

Then, lower, and colder than anything he's said from the throne: "You looked at him before you looked at me."

I did. Everyone in this hall does. I don't say so.

"Do you know senet?"

"No."

"Good." His eyes go, for half a second, to the Vizier's back, and come back to me. "Learn slowly."

That's all. He sits back, and the tired, frightened, nine-year-old face closes like a box and goes polite and empty.

I stand on the step with that in my ears. A playmate who loses is harmless. Nobody in this hall heard him tell me so.

I came up those steps thinking I'd found the one other person in the room as trapped as me. I was right.

***

"Hori." The Commander doesn't look at the soldier from the river when he says it, or at me. "The servants' rooms."

Hori takes me out. He doesn't drag me. He walks, and I follow, because I'm property now, and that's what property does.

As we pass between the columns, a man in a wig leans to the man beside him and says, not very quietly, "The Pharaoh's toy." The other one laughs into his hand.

I count. A hundred and twelve paces, two turns, three steps down into a corridor where the plaster is bare and the air smells of lamp oil and old bread. Servants' rooms. Close enough to the hall to be sent for.

He pushes a cedar door open with his foot. A room as narrow as a man's arms spread. A reed mat, a clay bowl of water, a window slit high up with the last of the light coming through it red and lying across the mat like a cut.

He shoves me in, not hard, and I sit down on the mat, because my legs have decided.

There's something under the edge of the mat. A sandal. A child's, leather, small enough that it wouldn't go on my foot now. One of a pair.

I look up. Hori is still in the doorway, watching me hold it. He's young, I see now. Twenty, maybe. A sunburnt nose. He isn't cruel. He's someone who stopped expecting things to go well a while ago and has got comfortable there.

He reaches into the pouch at his belt, takes out a flat loaf with ash on the bottom, and drops it on the mat beside me. The bread I ran for.

"That one lasted eleven days," he says.

He pulls the door shut. A bar goes across it, on the outside.

***

I sit on the mat in the dark with the sandal in my lap.

The bread sits where he dropped it. I'm hungrier than I've ever been in my life, and I don't touch it. I couldn't tell you why. Not yet.

My hand finds the wall beside the mat in the dark, at the height a lying child's hand would reach, and finds scratches in the plaster. Short ones, a thumbnail deep, in a row. I count them with my finger, because I can't not.

Ten. And an eleventh that stops halfway, as if something interrupted it.

The red bar of light climbs the wall and goes purple and goes out. Above me the gong sounds again, and when it's gone I can hear my own breathing, small and fast, a child's. The shirt is stiff with mud. There's myrrh in my hair and river on my skin and under it, I swear, faint, old smoke. From the books. From the house.

Under the door a line of lamplight comes and goes, slow, and stops, and goes on. They've put a watch on a toy's door. The last one lay here and listened to it too.

Far off, where nothing is, a grain of gold sand lets go of the glass.

I put my knees up and my arms round them. They're very small knees.

I think about the boy on the cherry road, on his backside on the wet pavement in his blue shirt, laughing with his head back.

I think about my own voice under the tree, saying it to nobody.

I wished for a simpler life… and the gods gave me a palace full of people who want me dead.
