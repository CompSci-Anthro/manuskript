The king raises one hand.

He doesn't stand. He doesn't say anything. He lifts his right hand off the lion's head on the arm of the throne, palm out, not high, the way you'd ask to go to the bathroom in a classroom where asking was frowned on.

The priest stops with his hand on the scale.

I've been in rooms that went quiet. This one does something else. Sixty grown men in gold collars take the same half-breath and hold it, and every one of them, I watch it happen, lets his eyes slide off the raised hand and down the steps to the foot of the dais, to find out what it means.

The Vizier doesn't move. He doesn't look up at the boy. He stands with his falcon staff and his rings and his three scars a side, and his face is pleasant and interested, like a man watching a child try to open a jar.

"Wait," the king says.

The voice is high and thin and shouldn't carry. It carries. It's the voice I've heard described by narrators in a dozen documentaries, and now it's just a boy's voice in a big room, and it wobbles.

"Majesty." The heavy man bows again, so deep I can see the sweat on the back of his neck. "Lord of the Two Lands. The weighing is the law. A foreigner with the enemy's—"

"I heard."

Two words. Flat. The way a child says *I heard* when he's been told to put his shoes on for the third time.

"Bring him up."

***

The hand on my neck lifts me and walks me forward, across the painted water, past the girl with the rag, who doesn't look up, until I'm standing at the bottom of the steps with my tied hands in front of me and the gold footstool with its painted prisoners at the level of my eyes.

Up close he's smaller still.

His wrists are sticks inside the gold. The false beard is too heavy for his face and pulls his chin down. Under the kohl there are shadows, the real kind, the kind you get from lying awake. He smells of myrrh and, under it, of something ordinary and warm, bread or milk, a little boy's smell.

He looks down at me from the throne of Egypt. I look back, and I don't lower my eyes.

I should. Everything I've learned in three years of offices and interviews says drop them, be small, be sorry. But I'm already small. I've never in my life been smaller. And I've just about run out of sorry.

So I look at him, and he looks at me. My muddy yellow hair. The strange shirt. The rope. The cut in my eyebrow.

Then he says, clearly, pitched to reach the back of the hall:

"I shall sentence you."

The priest's hand comes off the scale.

"By my royal authority," the king says, "I declare you to be my royal playmate. And pet."

***

For a second nobody gets it.

I don't get it. The words arrive the half-beat late they always do, and I have to hear them twice before they'll sit still.

*Playmate.*

The second one takes longer.

Then the hall gets it, and the sound it makes isn't loud, courtiers don't shout, but it goes down the columns like something spilled. Shock. A kind of disgust. Two short laughs, strangled off behind hands.

The heavy man has gone red to the wig. "Majesty, with respect. The boy is a foreigner. He is possibly a sorcerer. He cannot simply be *kept*, like a—"

"He is mine now."

"Majesty, the weighing—"

"Pharaoh has sentenced."

He says it louder. Not much. The voice breaks clean in half on *Pharaoh*, and his hand has gone back to the black scarab on his collar and shut on it, and I can see from here that it's shaking.

He's terrified.

He's nine years old and he's terrified and he's doing it anyway. And I feel something I haven't felt since I was fifteen and watching my own house burn, which is that I would like, very much, to put myself between somebody and a thing.

*He can't stop the weighing,* I think. *He isn't allowed. Everyone in this room knows he isn't allowed. So he's found the one sentence a king can pass that nobody can carry out with a knife, because you can't execute the king's property, and he's passed it before anyone could tell him no.*

*That isn't a child's trick. That's a lawyer's.*

The silence after *Pharaoh has sentenced* goes on long enough to hurt.

Every face in the hall turns, not fast, to the foot of the dais.

The Vizier lets them look. He lets the boy's hand shake on the scarab one more time. Then he bows, and it's a lovely thing, the staff dipping, the rings going down in a wave.

"The Lord of the Two Lands has spoken," he says softly, to the room, "and his word is the seal. A toy for the king." A pause. "Order before mercy. But a toy costs the Two Lands nothing."

He straightens.

He hasn't said a word to me, and he doesn't now. But as he comes up out of the bow, his eyes come to me and stay, and they narrow a little, the way a man's do when the print is small and he's decided it's worth the trouble of reading.

I've been looked at by a lot of adults. Teachers. Detectives. The uncle in Osaka, measuring how much of his spare room I'd take up. I know what it looks like when someone sees a kid.

This isn't it.

*Noted,* I think. *I've been noted.*

The scribe at the foot of the steps holds up my bill. The Vizier takes it between two linen-wrapped fingers and looks at the old man's face on it for exactly as long as he looked at mine.

"Enter it," he says, "and keep it with the records of this hall. Things from nowhere should be kept where they can be found again."

The scribe bows and lays my ten thousand yen in a cedar box, and shuts the lid.

The last piece of the world I came from, in a box, in a palace, and it was the Vizier who decided where.

"Nakhtmin."

The wall beside the throne moves. The Commander comes down the steps and stands over me, and I find out he's even bigger from below.

"Cut the rope."

"It will be done."

There's nothing in the voice. No heat, no cold. The curved sword comes out and goes down between my wrists before I've seen him draw, and the cord falls in two pieces, and I felt the bronze go by. It didn't touch skin. He was never going to let it.

He sheathes the sword and goes back up to his place and doesn't look at me again.

I rub my wrists. *That was a measurement,* I think. *He wanted to know if I'd flinch. I don't know yet whether it was good or bad that I didn't.*

***

The far doors bang.

A man comes in between the columns at a run that's become a stagger, grey with dust from his hair to his feet, two guards half holding him up. He reaches the edge of the lamplight, goes down on his face, kisses the painted reeds, and gets his message out before the herald has drawn breath.

"Majesty. From the north. The men of Hatti have come down again. Another city that sent tribute to Kemet has opened its gates to them, and its prince has sworn to their king. The road past it is shut."

The hall comes apart.

Now they shout. Men in gold push forward between the columns, all at once, all over each other.

*Hatti again. That's the third this year. Send the army. There's no army to send, the treasury's empty, the god's house has had it all for the building. Then take it back from the god's house. You'd take from Amun?*

The Vizier lifts his staff. Not high. A hand's width.

It stops.

"The council will hear the messenger," he says. "Privately. The king is tired."

Nobody asks the king if he's tired.

I've stopped listening to the Vizier. I'm standing at the foot of the dais with the rope off and mud drying on my legs, and my mother's chart is unrolling itself down the wall over my bed, with all her small notes in the margins.

*Letters from the vassal kings, begging. "Send archers." "Send archers." Nobody sends archers.*

*Suppiluliuma. The Hittite. Takes everything north of the river that the last two kings let slip.*

*The stela this boy puts up in the temple, with his own name on it. "If an army was sent to Djahy to widen the borders of Egypt, it had no success."*

*No success.*

I know this.

I don't know the day, or the city, or the name of the prince who just changed sides. But I know which way this reign leans, because I was raised by two people who had read the end of it. I know what gets carved on the walls and what gets cut off them afterward with chisels. I know the number at the bottom of the box.

Sixty men in this room are shouting about what happens next, and not one of them knows.

I do.

*A child can't hold a sword,* I think, and my hands have started shaking again, and it isn't fear this time. *A child can't sit on the council, or ask who wrote the order. But a child who knows what's coming—*

*—would be worth keeping. For a while.*

***

"Come here."

It's very quiet. It's for me.

The court is still loud over the messenger. Nobody is watching the steps. I go up two, three, until I'm beside the painted footstool and the gold sandals hanging above it, and the king of Egypt leans down off his father's throne until his face is near mine.

"What are you called?"

"Kael."

He tries it. "Kael." Carefully, like a word in somebody else's language. The sound of it in that voice goes through me, because it's the first time anyone in this whole world has said my name.

Then, lower:

"Do you know senet?"

"No."

"Good." His eyes go, for half a second, to the Vizier's back, and come back to me. "Learn slowly."

That's all. He sits back. The tired, frightened, nine-year-old face closes like a box and goes polite and empty, and he looks out over his shouting court as if I've been dismissed, and maybe I have.

I stand on the step with that in my ears.

*Learn slowly.*

He wants a playmate who loses. A playmate who loses is harmless, and a harmless thing is a thing nobody troubles to take away from a child. He's just told me how to stay alive, in three words, in front of all of them, and not one of them heard it, because who listens to a little boy asking his toy if it can play a game.

I came up those steps thinking I'd found the one other person in the room as trapped as me.

I was right. I just hadn't thought through what a trapped thing learns to do.

***

The soldier from the river takes me out.

He doesn't drag me. He walks, and I follow, because I'm property now, and that's what property does.

As we pass between the columns, a man in a wig leans to the man beside him and says, not very quietly, "The Pharaoh's toy." The other one laughs into his hand.

I count.

A hundred and twelve paces from the hall doors. Left at a painted pillar. Right past a court with a dry fountain. Down three steps into a corridor where the plaster is bare and the doors are plain, and the air smells of lamp oil and old bread.

Servants' rooms. Close enough to the hall to be sent for.

He stops at a cedar door and pushes it open with his foot. The room behind is as narrow as a man's arms spread. A reed mat. A clay bowl of water on the floor. A window slit high up, with the last of the light coming through it red and lying across the mat like a cut.

He puts a hand between my shoulders and shoves, not hard, and I go in and sit down on the mat, because my legs have decided.

There's something under the edge of the mat. I feel for it.

A sandal. A child's, leather, small enough that it wouldn't go on my foot now, which is saying something. One of a pair.

I look up. The soldier is still in the doorway, watching me hold it. He's young, I see now. Twenty, maybe. A sunburnt nose. Not cruel, I think. Just someone who stopped expecting things to go well a while ago and has got comfortable there.

"That one lasted eleven days," he says.

He pulls the door shut. A bar goes across it, on the outside.

***

I sit on the mat in the dark with the sandal in my lap.

The red bar of light climbs the wall and goes purple and goes out. Somewhere above me the gong sounds again, once, and the stone keeps it, and when it's finally gone I can hear my own breathing, which is small and fast and belongs to a child.

The blue shirt is stiff with mud. There's myrrh in my hair and river on my skin and under it, I swear, faint, old smoke. From the books. From the house.

*Eleven days.*

*Learn slowly.*

Somewhere in this palace a man with linen on his fingers has decided which box I belong in. Somewhere a priest is putting a feather away for tomorrow. Somewhere a girl is wringing out a rag. Somewhere a nine-year-old is lying in a bed built for his father, deciding, with his eyes open, how long his new toy is likely to last.

And somewhere a long way off, where nothing is, a man made of dust is watching a very small hourglass.

*Tick.*

I put my knees up and my arms round them. They're very small knees.

I think about the boy on the cherry road, on his backside on the wet pavement in his blue shirt, laughing with his head back.

I think about my own voice under the tree, saying it to nobody.

I wished for a simpler life…

…and the gods gave me a palace full of people who want me dead.
