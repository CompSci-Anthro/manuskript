The king raises one hand. Not high. Palm out, off the lion's head on the arm of the throne, the way you'd ask to go to the bathroom in a classroom where asking was frowned on.

The priest stops with his hand on the scale. Sixty grown men in gold collars hold the same half-breath, and every one of them lets his eyes slide off the raised hand and down the steps to the foot of the dais, to find out what it means.

The Vizier doesn't look up. His face is pleasant and interested, like a man watching a child try to open a jar.

"Wait," the king says.

The voice is high and thin and shouldn't carry. It carries. It wobbles.

"Majesty." The heavy man bows again. "The weighing is the law. A foreigner with the enemy's—"

"I heard."

The way a child says *I heard* when he's been told to put his shoes on for the third time.

"Bring him up."

***

The hand on my neck walks me forward until I'm standing at the bottom of the steps with the painted footstool at the level of my eyes.

Up close he's smaller still. His wrists are sticks inside the gold. Under the kohl there are shadows, the kind you get from lying awake. He smells of myrrh and, under it, of something ordinary and warm, bread or milk, a little boy's smell.

He looks down at me from the throne of Egypt, and I look back, and I don't lower my eyes. I should. Three years of offices and interviews say drop them, be small, be sorry. But I'm already small. And I've just about run out of sorry.

Then he says, clearly, pitched to reach the back of the hall:

"I shall sentence you."

The priest's hand comes off the scale.

"By my royal authority, I declare you to be my royal playmate. And pet."

***

For a second nobody gets it, me included.

Then the hall gets it, and the sound it makes isn't loud, courtiers don't shout, but it goes down the columns like something spilled. Shock. Disgust. Two short laughs, strangled off behind hands.

The heavy man has gone red to the wig. "Majesty, with respect. The boy is a foreigner. Possibly a sorcerer. He cannot simply be *kept*, like a—"

"He is mine now."

"Majesty, the weighing—"

"Pharaoh has sentenced."

The voice breaks clean in half on *Pharaoh*. His hand has gone back to the black scarab and shut on it, and I can see from here that it's shaking.

He's nine years old and he's terrified and he's doing it anyway, and I feel something I haven't felt since I was fifteen and watching my own house burn, which is that I would like, very much, to put myself between somebody and a thing.

*He can't stop the weighing; everyone in this room knows he isn't allowed. So he's found the one sentence a king can pass that nobody can carry out with a knife, because you can't execute the king's property.*

*That isn't a child's trick. That's a lawyer's.*

The silence after *Pharaoh has sentenced* goes on long enough to hurt. Every face in the hall turns, not fast, to the foot of the dais.

The Vizier lets the boy's hand shake on the scarab one more time. Then he bows, and it's a lovely thing, the staff dipping, the rings going down in a wave.

"The Lord of the Two Lands has spoken," he says softly, to the room, "and his word is the seal. A toy for the king. Order before mercy, but a toy costs the Two Lands nothing."

He hasn't said a word to me, and he doesn't now. But as he comes up out of the bow his eyes come to me and stay, and narrow a little, the way a man's do when the print is small and he's decided it's worth reading.

I know what it looks like when an adult sees a kid. This isn't it.

*Noted,* I think. *I've been noted.*

The scribe at the foot of the steps holds up my bill, and the Vizier takes it between two linen-wrapped fingers and looks at the old man's face on it for exactly as long as he looked at mine.

"Enter it, and keep it with the records of this hall. Things from nowhere should be kept where they can be found again."

The scribe lays my ten thousand yen in a cedar box and shuts the lid. The last piece of the world I came from, and it was the Vizier who decided where it went.

"Nakhtmin."

The Commander comes down the steps and stands over me.

"Cut the rope."

"It will be done."

There's nothing in the voice. The curved sword comes out and goes down between my wrists before I've seen him draw, and the cord falls in two pieces, and I felt the bronze go by. It didn't touch skin. He was never going to let it.

*That was a measurement,* I think. *He wanted to know if I'd flinch.*

***

The far doors bang.

A man comes in between the columns at a run that's become a stagger, grey with dust from hair to feet. He goes down on his face at the edge of the lamplight and gets his message out before the herald has drawn breath.

"Majesty. From the north. The men of Hatti have come down again. Another city that sent tribute to Kemet has opened its gates to them, and its prince has sworn to their king. The road past it is shut."

The hall comes apart. *Hatti again. That's the third this year. Send the army. There's no army to send; the god's house has had it all for the building. You'd take from Amun?*

The Vizier lifts his staff a hand's width, and it stops.

"The council will hear the messenger. Privately. The king is tired."

Nobody asks the king if he's tired.

I've stopped listening. My mother's chart is unrolling itself down the wall over my bed, with all her small notes in the margins.

*Letters from the vassal kings, begging for archers. Nobody sends archers.*

*The stela this boy puts up in the temple, with his own name on it. "If an army was sent to Djahy to widen the borders of Egypt, it had no success."*

I know this. Not the day, or the city, or the name of the prince who just changed sides. But I know which way this reign leans, because I was raised by two people who had read the end of it, and I know the number at the bottom of the box.

Sixty men in this room are shouting about what happens next, and not one of them knows.

I do.

*A child can't hold a sword,* I think, and my hands have started shaking again, and it isn't fear this time. *A child can't sit on the council, or ask who wrote the order. But a child who knows what's coming—*

*—would be worth keeping. For a while.*

***

"Come here."

It's very quiet. It's for me. Nobody is watching the steps. I go up two, three, until I'm beside the painted footstool, and the king of Egypt leans down off his father's throne until his face is near mine.

"What are you called?"

"Kael."

He tries it. "Kael." Carefully, like a word in somebody else's language, and the sound of it goes through me, because it's the first time anyone in this whole world has said my name.

Then, lower: "Do you know senet?"

"No."

"Good." His eyes go, for half a second, to the Vizier's back, and come back to me. "Learn slowly."

That's all. He sits back, and the tired, frightened, nine-year-old face closes like a box and goes polite and empty.

I stand on the step with that in my ears.

He wants a playmate who loses. A playmate who loses is harmless, and nobody troubles to take a harmless thing away from a child. He's just told me how to stay alive, in front of all of them, and not one of them heard it, because who listens to a little boy asking his toy if it can play a game.

I came up those steps thinking I'd found the one other person in the room as trapped as me. I was right. I just hadn't thought through what a trapped thing learns to do.

***

The soldier from the river takes me out. He doesn't drag me. He walks, and I follow, because I'm property now, and that's what property does.

As we pass between the columns, a man in a wig leans to the man beside him and says, not very quietly, "The Pharaoh's toy." The other one laughs into his hand.

I count. A hundred and twelve paces, two turns, three steps down into a corridor where the plaster is bare and the air smells of lamp oil and old bread. Servants' rooms. Close enough to the hall to be sent for.

He pushes a cedar door open with his foot. A room as narrow as a man's arms spread. A reed mat, a clay bowl of water, a window slit high up with the last of the light coming through it red and lying across the mat like a cut.

He shoves me in, not hard, and I sit down on the mat, because my legs have decided.

There's something under the edge of the mat. A sandal. A child's, leather, small enough that it wouldn't go on my foot now, which is saying something. One of a pair.

I look up. The soldier is still in the doorway, watching me hold it. He's young, I see now. Twenty, maybe. Not cruel. Just someone who stopped expecting things to go well a while ago.

"That one lasted eleven days," he says.

He pulls the door shut. A bar goes across it, on the outside.

***

I sit on the mat in the dark with the sandal in my lap.

The red bar of light climbs the wall and goes purple and goes out. Somewhere above me the gong sounds again, and when it's gone I can hear my own breathing, which is small and fast and belongs to a child. There's myrrh in my hair and river on my skin and under it, I swear, faint, old smoke. From the house.

*Eleven days.*

*Learn slowly.*

Somewhere in this palace a man with linen on his fingers has decided which box I belong in. Somewhere a nine-year-old is lying in a bed built for his father, deciding, with his eyes open, how long his new toy is likely to last.

And somewhere a long way off, where nothing is, a man made of dust is watching a very small hourglass.

*Tick.*

I put my knees up and my arms round them. They're very small knees.

I think about the boy on the cherry road, on his backside on the wet pavement in his blue shirt, laughing with his head back.

I think about my own voice under the tree, saying it to nobody.

I wished for a simpler life…

…and the gods gave me a palace full of people who want me dead.
