They say gods don't make mistakes.

I've had a lot of time to think about that line since, mostly at night, on a reed mat, in a palace where most people would prefer me dead by the end of the week. I've decided it's true, which is worse. A mistake you could appeal.

What I asked for was to be ten again.

What I got was a rope on my wrists, a hall full of gold, and a king younger than me deciding whether I'd see the morning.

But that's later. First there was a Tuesday, and a tree.

***

**Japan. Spring. A Tuesday.**

My parents were obsessed with history.

I mean that as a diagnosis, not a compliment. Other kids' fathers had a team. Mine had a dynasty. The Eighteenth, which he'd tell you was the greatest run of rulers any country has produced, and then he'd tell you why, and you'd miss your train.

My mother was quieter about it, but she was the one who taped the chart over my bed when I was six. Every king of the dynasty, and in her small handwriting, the year each one took the throne and the year he died. She didn't think a child needed the middle part.

I could read that chart before I could read a clock. I'm eighteen now and I still can't see the number nineteen without thinking *that's how old he was.*

That's the kind of house it was. It burned down three years ago, in March, at about two in the morning. Somebody poured something through the letter slot and lit it. The police said *arson* on the first day and *ongoing* for about a year after that, and then they stopped saying anything.

I was the one who got out.

***

The cherry road is not the fast way to school. It's the way you take if you don't want to see anyone from your class before the bell, and it costs you nine minutes. I timed it, the spring after the fire, when I needed to be in charge of one small thing.

I take it every day. Nine minutes is cheap.

The trees are old, and they lean in from both sides until the lane is a tunnel, and in the first week of April the tunnel is pink and the pavement is pink and the gutters are pink. It rained last night. The petals have glued themselves to the wet concrete in a pattern that looks like someone trying to spell something and giving up.

My phone buzzes in my blazer pocket. I don't take it out. I know the shape of what's on there. The class chat, counting down to the entrance exams. A mail from the university where my parents used to teach, confirming, with what I can only assume is a straight face, that they've received my application to study history.

And Soren. My twin brother writes on the first of every month from Osaka, where he lives with the uncle who offered to take me too and was visibly relieved when I said no. This month's message begins *It's three years on Sunday*, and that's all the preview shows, and that's all I've read.

Thirty-one steps from the corner to the first lamppost. I don't count on purpose anymore. It just happens.

My reflection keeps pace with me along the puddles. Black uniform, black hair that wants cutting, and a white line through the left eyebrow where a foil snapped at a tournament when I was twelve. The piece went past my eye close enough that I felt the air move. I stood at the mirror a long time that night, feeling lucky.

I'm not sure I've felt it since. I'd have to check.

***

"You're early."

Mrs. Hayashi runs the tea shop at the top of the slope and sweeps in front of it every morning, as if the petals were a private matter between her and the trees. She's about seventy and comes up to my shoulder. The broom is taller than she is.

"Couldn't sleep," I say.

"You never sleep. You're grey. Look at you." She looks at me, since I'm apparently not going to. Then she props the broom against the wall and wipes her hands on her apron, which is what she does before saying something she's rehearsed. "Your mother's books came. The order from Kyoto. Six volumes. I had them put in my name, after. I didn't know what else to do with the account."

Six books my mother ordered in February, three years ago, and talked about at dinner. I can't remember a single title, because I was fifteen and it was dinner.

I take out my wallet. There's one note in it, a ten-thousand-yen bill folded in half and in half again, which I've carried since New Year for a reason I've forgotten.

"How much was it?"

"Put that away. After school. Come in the back way and I'll give you tea, and you'll carry them home in a bag, not in the rain like a lunatic. Go."

I put the bill in the breast pocket of my blazer, over my heart, where I'll feel it and remember, and bow, and go on down the slope.

Behind me the broom starts again. It has a rhythm like someone breathing in their sleep.

***

The oldest tree is halfway down.

It's twice the girth of the others, and its roots have come up through the pavement and split around a little stone shrine the size of a mailbox, with a door sealed in grey mortar before anyone alive can remember. Over the door there's a mark: a circle with one line through it, straight down from the top. I used to think it was a clock face with the hands gone.

Mrs. Hayashi sweeps the shrine too. I asked her why once and she said, "Somebody should."

Every morning the old tree drops the same low branch across the pavement. I step over it.

Across the lane, a knot of kids from the elementary school are racing to the corner, yellow caps bobbing. The smallest, a boy of nine or ten, is trying to catch petals in his mouth and failing, because he's laughing too hard to close it.

He's wearing a blue shirt with red sleeves.

I had that shirt. My mother bought it in a museum gift shop the summer I turned ten, the trip where my father stood me in front of the gold mask and said, "He was king when he was your age, Kael. Nine. Think about that." I thought about it so hard I had a nightmare.

The boy trips, sits down hard on the wet pavement, and laughs even louder, head back, with his whole body.

I'd give a great deal, right now, to laugh like that about anything.

They go round the corner and the lane is quiet, and my phone buzzes again, and I'm so tired of being the one who's left that I say it out loud without quite deciding to.

"I wish I was ten again. Life was so much simpler."

The petals stop falling.

All of them. A few thousand points of pink hanging in the air between me and the trees, the way a photograph hangs. One is a hand's width from my face. I can see the little notch at the end of it.

I think, very clearly: *that isn't how anything works.*

**CRACK.**

It comes up through the soles of my shoes. The shrine's mortar splits with a sound like a knuckle. The roots lift the pavement in a slab, and the oldest tree on the cherry road tears loose from the ground and leans. Slow at first.

Then not slow.

I see it coming. Every coach I ever had said that about me, that I see the attack before it starts.

Seeing it is not the same as moving.

*Of course,* I think. *Of course it's this one.*

The trunk comes down. It doesn't hurt. It's just weight, an impossible amount of it, and the smell of wet bark, and a long way off, up the hill, the sound of a broom stopping.

Somewhere under me, my phone is still buzzing.

Then it's not.
