They say gods don't make mistakes.

I've been turning that line over all night, on a reed mat, in a palace where most people would prefer me dead by the end of the week. I've decided it's true, which is worse. A mistake you could appeal.

What I asked for was to be ten again.

What I got was a rope on my wrists, a hall full of gold, and a king younger than me deciding whether I'd see the morning.

But start with the tree.

***

**Japan. Spring. A Tuesday.**

My parents were obsessed with history.

I mean that as a diagnosis, not a compliment. Other kids' fathers had a team. Mine had a dynasty. The Eighteenth, and he'd tell you why, and you'd miss your train.

My mother was quieter about it, but she was the one who taped the chart over my bed when I was six. Every king of the dynasty, and in her small handwriting, the year each one took the throne and the year he died. She didn't think a child needed the middle part.

I'm eighteen now and I still can't see the number nineteen without thinking *that's how old he was.*

That's the kind of house it was. It burned down three years ago, in March, at two in the morning. Somebody poured something through the letter slot and lit it. The police said *arson*, and then *ongoing*, and then nothing.

I was the one who got out.

***

The cherry road costs nine minutes more than the fast way to school, and it saves you everyone from your class.

The old trees lean in from both sides until the lane is a tunnel, and in the first week of April the tunnel is pink. It rained last night.

My phone buzzes in my blazer pocket. I don't take it out. The class chat, counting down to the exams. And Soren, my twin, who writes on the first of every month from Osaka. This month's message begins *It's three years on Sunday*, and that's all I've read.

My reflection keeps pace with me along the puddles. Black uniform, black hair that wants cutting, a white line through the left eyebrow where a foil snapped at a tournament when I was twelve.

***

"You're early."

Mrs. Hayashi runs the tea shop at the top of the slope and sweeps in front of it every morning. The broom is taller than she is.

"Couldn't sleep," I say.

"You're grey." She props the broom against the wall, which is what she does before saying something she's rehearsed. "Your mother's books came. Six volumes. I had them put in my name, after."

Six books my mother ordered three years ago and talked about at dinner. I can't remember a single title, because I was fifteen and it was dinner.

I take out the one note in my wallet, a ten-thousand-yen bill folded in half and in half again.

"How much was it?"

"Put that away. After school. Come in the back way and I'll give you tea. Go."

I put the bill in the breast pocket of my blazer, over my heart, where I'll feel it and remember. Six books: one more line in the ledger I keep of what I owe. I bow and go on down the slope.

***

The oldest tree is halfway down.

It's twice the girth of the others, and its roots have come up through the pavement and split around a little stone shrine the size of a mailbox, its door sealed in grey mortar before anyone alive can remember. Mrs. Hayashi sweeps its step every morning on her way back up. Over the door there's a mark: a circle with one line through it, straight down from the top.

Every morning the old tree drops the same low branch across the pavement. I step over it.

Across the lane, kids from the elementary school are racing to the corner, yellow caps bobbing. The smallest, a boy of nine or ten, is trying to catch petals in his mouth and failing, because he's laughing too hard to close it.

He's wearing a blue shirt with red sleeves.

I had that shirt. My mother bought it in a museum gift shop the summer I turned ten, the trip where my father stood me in front of the gold mask and said, "He was king when he was your age, Kael. Nine. Think about that."

The boy trips, sits down hard on the wet pavement, and laughs even louder, head back, with his whole body.

I'd give a great deal, right now, to laugh like that about anything.

The lane goes quiet behind them, and my phone buzzes again, and I'm so tired of being the one who's left that I say it out loud without quite deciding to.

"I wish I was ten again. Things were so much simpler."

The petals stop falling.

All of them. A few thousand points of pink hanging in the air between me and the trees. One is a hand's width from my face. I can see the little notch at the end of it.

I think, very clearly: *that isn't how anything works.*

**CRACK.**

It comes up through the soles of my shoes. The shrine's mortar splits with a sound like a knuckle. The roots lift the pavement in a slab, and the oldest tree on the cherry road tears loose from the ground and leans. Slow at first.

Then not slow.

I see it coming. Every coach I ever had said that about me.

Seeing it is not the same as moving.

*Of course,* I think. *Of course it's this one.*

The trunk comes down. It doesn't hurt. It's just weight, an impossible amount of it, and the smell of wet bark, and a long way off, up the hill, the sound of a broom stopping.

Somewhere under me, my phone is still buzzing.

Then it's not.
