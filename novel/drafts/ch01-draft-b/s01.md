They say gods don't make mistakes.

I've had a lot of time to think about that line since. Mostly at night, on a reed mat, in a palace where most people would prefer me dead by the end of the week. I've decided it's true, which is worse. A mistake you could appeal.

What I asked for was to be ten again.

What I got was a rope on my wrists, a hall full of gold, and a king younger than me deciding whether I'd see the morning.

But that's later. First there was a Tuesday, and a tree.

***

**Japan. Spring. A Tuesday.**

My parents were obsessed with history.

I mean that as a diagnosis, not a compliment. Other kids' fathers had a team. Mine had a dynasty. The Eighteenth, specifically, which he'd tell you was the greatest run of rulers any country has produced, and then he'd tell you why, and you'd miss your train.

My mother was quieter about it, but she was the one who taped the chart over my bed when I was six. Ahmose at the top, Horemheb at the bottom, and all the way down, in her small handwriting, the dates. Not the dates they were born. The dates they came to the throne and the dates they died. She didn't think a child needed the middle part.

I could read that chart before I could read a clock. I'm eighteen now and I still can't see the number nineteen without thinking *that's how old he was.*

Anyway. That's the kind of house it was.

It burned down three years ago, in March, at about two in the morning. Somebody poured something through the letter slot and lit it. The police used the word *arson* on the first day and the word *ongoing* for about a year after that, and then they stopped using words.

I was the one who got out.

***

The cherry road is not the fast way to school. It's the way you take if you don't want to see anyone from your class before the bell, and it costs you about nine minutes.

I know because I timed it, the spring after the fire, with the stopwatch on my phone. That's the kind of thing you do when you need to be in charge of one small thing.

I take it every day now. Nine minutes is cheap.

The trees are old. They lean in from both sides until the lane is a tunnel, and in the first week of April the tunnel is pink, and the pavement under it is pink, and the gutters are pink. A stranger would say it's beautiful. It is. I've just seen it enough that it's also only the way to school.

It rained last night. The petals have glued themselves to the wet concrete in a pattern that looks like someone trying to spell something and giving up.

My bag is heavy on one shoulder, mostly past-paper books, with my fencing jacket folded on top because I keep meaning to return it to the club and keep not doing it.

My phone goes off in my blazer pocket. One short buzz, then another.

I don't take it out. I know the shape of what's on there without looking. The class group chat doing its countdown to the entrance exams. A mail from the university where my parents used to teach, confirming, with what I can only assume is a straight face, that they've received my application to study history.

And Soren.

My brother writes on the first of the month. He's in Osaka with our uncle, who offered to take me too and was visibly relieved when I said no. Soren's message this month begins *It's three years on Sunday*, and that's all the preview shows, and that's all I've read.

Thirty-one steps from the corner to the first lamppost. I don't do this on purpose anymore. It just happens.

My reflection keeps pace with me along the puddles. Black uniform with the collar hooked. Black hair that wants cutting. A face my homeroom teacher once described in a report as *composed*, which is a word teachers use when they can't tell what you're thinking and have decided that's your fault.

There's a white line through my left eyebrow where a foil snapped at a tournament when I was twelve. The piece went past my eye close enough that I felt the air move. I stood at the mirror a long time that night, feeling lucky.

I'm not sure I've felt it since. Lucky. I'd have to check.

***

"You're early."

Mrs. Hayashi runs the tea shop at the top of the slope, and she sweeps in front of it every morning in a pale blue apron, as if the petals were a private matter between her and the trees. She's about seventy and comes up to my shoulder. The broom is taller than she is.

"Couldn't sleep," I say.

"You never sleep. You're grey. Look at you."

She looks at me, since I'm apparently not going to. Then she props the broom against the wall and wipes her hands on the apron, which is what she does before saying something she's rehearsed.

"Your mother's books came."

I don't say anything.

"The order from the dealer in Kyoto. Six volumes. He had trouble finding the fourth." She says it briskly, like a shopkeeper, which is kind of her. "I had them put in my name, after. I didn't know what else to do with the account. They're in the back. I'll wrap them."

Six books. My mother ordered them in February, three years ago, and talked about them at dinner, and I can't remember a single title, because I was fifteen and it was dinner.

I take out my wallet. There's one note in it, a ten-thousand-yen bill folded in half and in half again, which I've carried since New Year for a reason I've since forgotten.

"How much was it?"

"Put that away."

"Mrs. Hayashi."

"After school," she says. "Come in the back way and I'll give you tea, and you'll carry them home properly in a bag, not in the rain like a lunatic. Go."

I put the bill in the breast pocket of my blazer, behind my student ID, over my heart, where I'll feel it and remember. Then I bow to her, properly, and she flaps a hand at me, and I go on down the slope.

Behind me the broom starts again. It has a rhythm like someone breathing in their sleep.

***

The oldest tree is halfway down.

It's twice the girth of the others and leans so far over the lane that the city put a yellow sign on it once, years ago, and then apparently thought better of cutting it down. The roots have come up through the pavement and split around a little stone shrine at the base, the size of a mailbox, with a door that was sealed with grey mortar before anyone alive can remember.

There's a mark cut over the door. A circle with one line through it, straight down from the top. I used to think it was a clock face with the hands gone. My mother, when I was small and asked, said she wasn't sure, and I remember being surprised, because she was sure about everything.

Mrs. Hayashi sweeps the shrine too. Every morning. I asked her why once and she said, "Somebody should," which isn't an answer.

Every morning the old tree drops the same low branch across the pavement, and every morning somebody has to step over it.

I step over it.

Across the lane, a knot of kids from the elementary school are racing each other to the corner. Yellow caps bobbing, somebody's bag coming open, everyone shrieking. The smallest one, a boy of nine or ten, is trying to catch petals in his mouth and failing, mostly because he's laughing too hard to close it.

He's wearing a blue shirt with red sleeves.

I had that shirt. Not one like it. That one. My mother bought it in a museum gift shop the summer I turned ten, because I'd got chocolate ice cream down the front of the one I came in, and I wore it until the sleeves were halfway up my forearms.

That was the trip where my father stood me in front of the gold mask and said, "He was king when he was your age, Kael. Nine years old. Think about that." And I thought about it so hard I had a nightmare.

The boy in the blue shirt trips over his own feet, sits down hard on the wet pavement, and laughs even louder, head back, with his whole body. His friends run back to haul him up.

I'd give a great deal, right now, to laugh like that about anything.

They go around the corner and the lane is quiet. My phone buzzes again. I can feel the folded bill in my pocket, and under it my heart doing its ordinary work, and I'm so tired of being the one who's left that I say it out loud without quite deciding to.

"I wish I was ten again. Life was so much simpler."

The branches over my head stop moving.

I don't mean they go still. There's no wind to stop. I mean the petals that were falling stop falling, all of them, a few thousand points of pink hanging in the air between me and the trees, the way a photograph hangs. One is a hand's width from my face. I can see the little notch at the end of it.

I think, very clearly: *that isn't how anything works.*

**CRACK.**

It comes up through the soles of my shoes. The shrine's mortar splits with a sound like a knuckle. The roots lift the pavement in a slab, and the oldest tree on the cherry road tears loose from the ground and leans. Slow at first.

Then not slow.

I see it coming. Every coach I ever had said that about me, that I see the attack before it starts.

Seeing it is not the same as moving.

*Of course,* I think. *Of course it's this one.*

The trunk comes down.

It doesn't hurt. It's just weight, an impossible amount of it, and the smell of wet bark and rain, and a long way off, up the hill, the sound of a broom stopping.

Somewhere under me, my phone is still buzzing.

Then it's not.
